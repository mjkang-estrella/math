                                                                                               Abstract
                                                     Threshold graphs — the {P4 , C4 , 2K2 }-free graphs, equivalently those built from a single ver-
                                                 tex by repeatedly adding isolated or dominating vertices — are among the most thoroughly stud-
                                                 ied graph classes in spectral graph theory. This survey collects results on the spectrum of thresh-
                                                 old graphs across the full range of matrices studied in the literature: the adjacency, Laplacian,
                                                 and signless Laplacian matrices; the distance, distance Laplacian, and distance signless Lapla-
                                                 cian matrices; the eccentricity and Seidel matrices; and the normalized adjacency/Laplacian
                                                 matrix — together with the associated graph energies, cospectrality questions, extremal spec-
                                                 tral radius problems, and several generalizations (weighted, quasi-threshold, hypergraph, and
                                                 random threshold graphs). We emphasize results from the recent years, including the foun-
                                                 dational work of Chvátal–Hammer, Merris, Bapat, and Jacobs–Lazzarin–Trevisan–Tura. We
                                                 also present a study of integral threshold graphs (those with all-integer spectrum) across all
                                                 seven matrices. We close with a list of open problems and conjectures drawn directly from the
                                                 surveyed literature and from this new analysis.
                                                 AMS Subject Classification: 05C50, 05-02, 15-02.
                                                 Keywords: Threshold graphs, graph matrices-adjacency, Laplacian, signless Laplacian, dis-
                                                 tance, distance Laplacian, distance signless Laplacian, eccentricity, Seidel, normalized adjacency;
                                                 cospectral, eigenvalue-free interval, integral graphs.


                                           1    Introduction
                                           A threshold graph is a finite simple graph that can be built from a single vertex by repeatedly per-
                                           forming one of two operations: adding a new isolated vertex (adjacent to nothing already present),
                                           or adding a new dominating vertex (adjacent to everything already present). The resulting sequence
                                           of choices, written as a binary string b1 b2 b3 · · · bn ∈ {0, 1}n−1 (vertex i isolated if bi = 0, dominating
                                           if bi = 1), is called the creation sequence of the graph, and completely determines it up to the
                                           labeling of vertices [21].
                                               Threshold graphs admit several equivalent characterizations that explain both their name and
                                           their ubiquity:


                                                                                                   1
   • Forbidden subgraphs. G is a threshold graph if and only if it contains no induced P4 (path
     on four vertices), C4 (cycle on four vertices), or 2K2 (two disjoint edges) [21].

   • Degree-threshold property. There exists a real weighting w : V (G) → R and a threshold
     t such that uv ∈ E(G) if and only if w(u) + w(v) > t — the origin of the name.

   • Nested neighborhoods. The vertices can be linearly ordered so that the neighborhoods
     (outside the vertex itself) are nested, which is why threshold graphs are also called nested
     split graphs [48].

   • Extremal degree sequences. Threshold graphs are exactly the graphs whose degree se-
     quence is not majorized by any other graphic sequence of the same length and sum — the
     maximal graphs in the majorization order on graphic sequences, a fact underlying their role
     in Laplacian spectral extremal problems (Section 6).
    Threshold graphs form a proper subclass of split graphs (vertex set partitions into a clique and
an independent set), cographs (P4 -free graphs, built by disjoint union and join), and interval graphs.
Within the broader cograph/split-graph hierarchy, threshold graphs are the unique graphs obtain-
able by a construction using only the join operation at every step (never a plain disjoint union)
when cographs are built from single vertices — equivalently, threshold graphs are exactly the totally
ordered cographs in the natural partial order on creation sequences. Numerous applications are doc-
umented in [48], ranging from scheduling and synchronization in computer science to psychological
scaling.

1.1   A field still very much in motion
Although the adjacency and Laplacian spectral theory of threshold graphs is largely complete —
settled across the 1990s and 2010s by Merris, Bapat, and the Jacobs–Trevisan–Tura program — the
class continues to generate active research at essentially every matrix surveyed in this paper, with
multiple results appearing as recently as 2025, 2026 and 2027. Three threads illustrate this well.
First, the question of exactly how few distinct eigenvalues a threshold graph’s spectrum can contain
has only just been settled in full generality: Allem, Hoppen, Lazzarin, Sibemberg, and Tura proved
in 2025 that every connected threshold graph on n vertices admits, for every n, some threshold graph
with at most four distinct adjacency eigenvalues [7] — a clean, order-independent bound obtained
only after a decade of finer-grained classification results for graphs with exactly two, three, four, or
five distinct eigenvalues (Section 3.4). A companion line of work by Allem, Oliveira, and Tura instead
asks which eigenvalues can be excluded entirely from a threshold graph’s spectrum, giving explicit
generating procedures for eigenvalue-free families [8]. The inverse problem for integer indices looks
into whether graphs with a given integer as their indices exist. This problem is solved very recently
by Schmidt et al. [59] within the class of threshold graphs. Second, the distance-based spectral
theory — distance, distance Laplacian, and (as detailed in Section 7.3) still-open distance signless
Laplacian spectra — has seen two independent, essentially simultaneous closed-form treatments
of the distance Laplacian spectrum appear within the same year: Lather, Gupta, and Balaji [43]
derive it via the master identity and Merris’s theorem, obtaining sharp cospectrality and extremal-
coefficient consequences, while Megha and Parvathy [51] derive it independently via the equitable
partition directly (Theorems 7.4–7.5 below); this sits alongside continuing, separate work on the
plain distance spectrum and when non-isomorphic threshold graphs can share one [47, 45]. Third,
the Seidel spectral theory, initiated by Del-Vecchio and Abdón [25] and substantially extended by
Mandal and Mehatari [49], exemplifies a pattern — a foundational classification followed, often
within one or two years, by a more detailed structural and cospectrality analysis — that recurs

                                                   2
across nearly every matrix treated in this survey. We have organized the paper so that each section
reflects this same historical arc: a foundational closed-form or classification result, followed by the
sharper, more structural or more recent results it enabled.

1.2   A new contribution: integral threshold graphs and DQ spectrum
Integral threshold graphs:
Beyond compiling the literature above, Section 13 presents a uniform study of integrality (all eigen-
values being integers across all seven matrices). The twin-class and master-identity mechanisms
responsible for Table 1’s cospectrality dichotomy turn out to govern integrality in strikingly paral-
lel fashion. We show that the Laplacian and distance Laplacian are integral for every connected
threshold graph, a direct consequence of Merris’s theorem (Theorem 4.1) and the master identity
(Proposition 7.3) that does not appear to have been stated explicitly for DL before. For the re-
maining five matrices — adjacency, Seidel, distance, distance signless Laplacian, and eccentricity
— no such unconditional statement holds, and we instead give a complete characterization for the
simplest family of threshold graphs (the complete split graphs CS(s, t)): the eccentricity matrix
turns out to be integral for every such graph via an exact block-diagonal mechanism, while the other
four reduce to explicit perfect-square-discriminant (Diophantine) conditions on (s, t), each satisfied
by infinitely many non-trivial instances. For general m ≥ 2, we prove that the eccentricity matrix’s
last block always splits off cleanly regardless of its size (Theorem 13.5), reducing but not resolv-
ing the problem, and we pose several concrete conjectures throughout the paper — on the residual
m = 2 eccentricity condition (Conjecture 13.6), and on a master-identity criterion (Conjecture 13.9)
unifying this section’s integrality dichotomy with Section 12’s cospectrality dichotomy — alongside
Open Problem 15.9 for the fully general case.
Distance signless Laplacian spectrum:
We compute the local eigenvalues of DQ in terms of the vertex transmission (see Theorem 7.8). The
closed form of the corresponding quotient matrix is defined in Theorem 7.9. The detail discussion
can be found in Section 7.3.

1.3   Why threshold graphs are spectrally tractable
The nested-neighborhood structure makes threshold graphs an unusually favorable setting for spec-
tral computation, for three structural reasons that recur throughout this survey:

  1. Twin classes. Grouping consecutive equal bits of the creation sequence into maximal runs
     0s1 1t1 0s2 1t2 · · · 0sm 1tm partitions the vertex set into 2m classes, within each of which all vertices
     are twins (true twins — identical closed neighborhoods — within a dominating run; false twins
     — identical open neighborhoods — within an isolated run). Twin vertices u, v always give
     an explicit eigenvector eu − ev for whichever graph matrix is under study, with an eigenvalue
     read off directly from the local structure. This is the source of the large-multiplicity, exactly
     computable local eigenvalues found for every matrix surveyed below.

  2. The equitable partition / quotient matrix. The same 2m-class partition is equitable for
     every matrix considered here (adjacency, Laplacian, signless Laplacian, distance-type, Seidel,
     eccentricity, normalized), because row sums into each class depend only on the class of the
     source vertex. This yields a 2m × 2m (or smaller) quotient matrix whose eigenvalues are
     exactly the remaining, generically irrational eigenvalues of the full n × n matrix — reducing
     an n-dimensional eigenvalue problem to one of size 2m ≪ n, and this is the single most-used
     technique across the entire literature surveyed here.

                                                      3
    3. Diameter at most 2. Every connected threshold graph with at least one dominating vertex
       has diameter at most 2, so its distance matrix is simply D(G) = 2(J − I) − A(G). This is what
       makes the distance, distance Laplacian, and distance signless Laplacian spectra of threshold
       graphs tractable at all, and is responsible for the clean master identity connecting the distance
       Laplacian spectrum to the ordinary Laplacian spectrum (Section 7.2).

1.4    Organization
We proceed matrix by matrix, broadly in the order these theories were historically developed for
threshold graphs: adjacency (Section 3, including the number-of-distinct-eigenvalues theme of Sec-
tion 3.4), Laplacian (Section 4), signless Laplacian and main eigenvalues (Section 5), graph energies
(Section 6), distance-type matrices — distance, distance Laplacian, distance signless Laplacian
(Section 7) — the eccentricity matrix (Section 8), the Seidel matrix (Section 9), the normalized
adjacency/Laplacian matrix (Section 10), extremal spectral radius problems (Section 11), cospec-
trality questions throughout (Section 12), our new study of integral threshold graphs (Section 13),
and generalizations — weighted, quasi-threshold, hypergraph, and random threshold graphs (Sec-
tion 14). We close with open problems and conjectures (Section 15) collected directly from the
surveyed papers and from Section 13’s new analysis.

3     The adjacency spectrum
The adjacency spectral theory of threshold graphs is the oldest and most completely developed part
of the subject, due largely to a sequence of papers by Jacobs, Trevisan, Tura, and others, building
on Bapat’s foundational 2013 paper.

3.1    Inertia and determinant
Theorem 3.1 (Bapat [19]). Let G be a threshold graph with creation sequence b2 · · · bn . Then the
inertia (number of negative, zero, and positive eigenvalues) of A(G) is determined explicitly by the

                                                   4
binary string: the number of negative eigenvalues equals the number of 1’s in the string, and the
nullity equals the number of 0’s that are immediately preceded by another 0 (or are the very first bit).
Bapat also gives a closed-form expression for det A(G) and, when it is nonsingular, for A(G)−1 .
    This single result — proved via the same equitable-partition / twin-vertex machinery outlined
in Section 1 — underlies essentially all subsequent inertia-type results for other matrices associated
with threshold graphs.

3.2   Eigenvalue location and the characteristic polynomial
Theorem 3.2 (Jacobs–Trevisan–Tura [38]). Every eigenvalue of A(G) other than 0 and −1 is
simple. Moreover, a threshold graph has no eigenvalue in the open interval (−1, 0).
    Because 0 and −1 are the only eigenvalues that can have multiplicity (arising respectively from
twins in isolated runs and from twins in dominating runs — exactly the local eigenvalues of Sec-
tion 1), they are regarded as trivial ; all further eigenvalue-location results are stated for the non-
trivial spectrum.

3.3   Eigenvalue distribution and a resolved conjecture
Theorem 3.6 (Jacobs–Trevisan–Tura [40]). Writing n− (G) and n+ (G) for the number of negative
and positive adjacency eigenvalues, almost every threshold graph (under the uniform random model
on creation sequences) satisfies n− (G) > n+ (G); in fact n− (G) > n+ (G) for every connected
threshold graph.
Theorem 3.7 (Lou–Wang–Huang [36]). The precise distribution of eigenvalues of a threshold graph
— how many lie in each of several natural intervals determined by the creation sequence — is
characterized completely.
   A conjecture on the ordering/location of threshold-graph eigenvalues, posed in the Jacobs–
Trevisan–Tura line of work, was resolved by Tura:
Theorem 3.8 (Tura [63]). The conjecture of [40] on the eigenvalues of threshold graphs holds;
moreover the anti-regular graph Hn is identified as the extremal case achieving the smallest positive
and largest (in absolute value) non-trivial negative eigenvalue among all threshold graphs on n
vertices.

                                                   5
3.4    The number of distinct eigenvalues
A natural refinement of eigenvalue-location questions is to ask not where eigenvalues can lie, but
how few distinct values they can take. Since threshold graphs on n vertices can have diameter and
radius bounded independently of n, classical bounds relating the number of distinct eigenvalues to
the diameter do not by themselves give a constant bound — yet one holds regardless:

Theorem 3.9 (Allem–Hoppen–Lazzarin–Sibemberg–Tura [7]). The minimum number of distinct
adjacency eigenvalues of a connected threshold graph on n ≥ 2 vertices is at most 4, for every n.
That is, minG s(A(G)) ≤ 4, where the minimum is taken over all connected threshold graphs on n
vertices.

    This is a striking, order-independent bound: however large n is, some threshold graph on n
vertices has at most 4 distinct adjacency eigenvalues, extending the finite classifications of threshold
graphs with exactly 2, 3, 4, or 5 distinct eigenvalues found in the earlier literature (Sections 3–9
record several such classification results for other matrices) into a genuine asymptotic statement
about the adjacency matrix.

3.5    The anti-regular graph as organizing principle
Theorem 3.10 (Aguilar–Ficarra–Schurman–Sullivan [2]). The anti-regular graph Hn plays a dis-
tinguished structural role in the adjacency spectral analysis of every threshold graph: every threshold
graph’s adjacency spectrum can be understood via comparison with, or construction from, the spec-
tral data of Hn . The authors pose the question of whether an analogous distinguished graph exists
for the wider class of cographs.

   A related tridiagonal-reduction technique, applicable to the adjacency (and other) quotient
matrices of threshold graphs and several related graph classes, is developed by Andelić, Du, da
Fonseca, and Simić [9].

3.6    Random threshold graphs
Recent work has also considered threshold graphs built by a random creation sequence (e.g. a
Pólya-urn-style process), deriving the exact degree distribution of an arbitrary vertex (including
mean and variance) and an asymptotic distribution of the adjacency and Laplacian eigenvalues
as n → ∞, together with an explicit closed-form description of the Laplacian eigenbasis in this
random setting. This complements the deterministic, creation-sequence-based results above with a
probabilistic large-n picture.


4     The Laplacian spectrum
The Laplacian spectrum of threshold graphs is exceptionally clean: it is always integral, and is given
exactly by the conjugate of the degree sequence.

Theorem 4.1 (Merris [52]). Let G be a threshold graph with degree sequence d1 ≥ · · · ≥ dn and
conjugate partition d∗ = (d∗1 , . . . , d∗n ). Then spec(L(G)) = {d∗1 , . . . , d∗n } exactly (as a multiset).
Equivalently, threshold graphs are exactly the graphs for which the Grone–Merris majorization in-
equality spec(L(G)) ⪯ d∗ holds with equality.




                                                      6
    This single theorem is the foundation for the entire Laplacian (and, via the master identity
of Section 7.2, distance Laplacian) theory of threshold graphs, and is a special case of the later
Grone–Merris–Bai theorem for general graphs.

Theorem 4.2 (Hammer et al. [31]). The Laplacian spectrum of a threshold graph determines,
and is determined by, the number of spanning trees and a range of further structural invariants,
computed explicitly from the creation sequence.

    A corresponding explicit eigenbasis (not just the eigenvalue multiset) for the Laplacian of a
threshold graph is constructed in the Laplacian-eigenbasis literature building on [53], giving explicit
eigenvectors for every conjugate-partition eigenvalue, not merely the local twin-difference vectors.

4.1   Weighted threshold graphs
Theorem 4.3 (Andelić–Stanić [10]). For a weighted threshold graph (edge/vertex weights respecting
the threshold structure), the Laplacian spectrum again equals the conjugate partition of an associated
weighted Ferrers diagram, giving a closed formula for the Laplacian spectrum in the weighted setting
— a direct and complete generalization of Theorem 4.1.

   This 2025 result closes a natural generalization question that had been open since the unweighted
case was settled by Merris three decades earlier.

4.2   Laplacian eigenvectors
Beyond the eigenvalue multiset, recent work gives an explicit combinatorial description of a full
Laplacian eigenbasis for threshold graphs directly from the creation sequence, refining the classical
eigenvector theory of Merris [53] to the threshold case specifically and relating the eigenvector
supports to the run structure of the creation sequence.


5     The signless Laplacian spectrum and main eigenvalues
The signless Laplacian Q(G) = Deg(G) + A(G) of a threshold graph does not enjoy an analogue
of Theorem 4.1 — its spectrum is generically irrational — but a remarkable interlacing property
holds instead.

Theorem 5.1 (Helmberg–Porto–Torres–Trevisan [35]). For a threshold graph G with degree se-
quence d1 ≥ d2 ≥ · · · ≥ dn , the eigenvalues q1 ≥ q2 ≥ · · · ≥ qn of Q(G) interlace the degree
sequence:

5.1   Main eigenvalues
An eigenvalue of a symmetric matrix M associated with G is called main if its eigenspace is not
orthogonal to the all-ones vector 1.

Theorem 5.3 (Vinagre–Trevisan–Bolckau–Chimelli [64]). Threshold graphs with exactly k main
Q-eigenvalues, for every k ≥ 2, are characterized explicitly, together with a recursive construction
producing all threshold graphs with k + 1 main Q-eigenvalues from those with k.

   This characterization is extended to the broader class of quasi-threshold graphs (a superclass
within the cographs):

Theorem 5.4 (Jones–Trevisan [41]; Jones–Trevisan–Vinagre [42]). The structural characterization
of Theorem 5.3 is generalized to connected quasi-threshold graphs with k ≥ 2 main Q-eigenvalues,
via a condensed signless Laplacian matrix of reduced size obtained from the same equitable-partition
reduction used for ordinary threshold graphs; the case k = 2 is resolved completely, and partial
results are given for k = 3, including for several subclasses such as trees, unicyclic, and bicyclic
quasi-threshold graphs.

    An analogous question for main eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix has also been studied for
the related class of chain graphs (the bipartite analogue of threshold graphs), extending the main-
eigenvalue program beyond the signless Laplacian setting.


6     Graph energies
The energy of a graph matrix is the sum of the absolute values of its eigenvalues (for the Lapla-
cian, deviations from the average degree are used instead). Threshold graphs play a distinguished
extremal role in this theory.

6.1   Adjacency energy
Theorem 6.1 (Jacobs–Trevisan–Tura [40]). Explicit bounds on the (adjacency) energy of threshold
graphs are given, and hyperenergetic threshold graphs (energy exceeding n) are exhibited for every
n > 8. Sequences of pairwise non-cospectral threshold graphs, all equienergetic to a common reference
graph, are also constructed.

6.2   Laplacian energy and Brouwer-type extremality
The Laplacian energy LE(G) = i |µi − 2m/n| (where µi are the Laplacian eigenvalues and 2m/n
                                P
the average degree) has a long history of extremal results placing threshold graphs at the center.

Theorem 6.2 (Helmberg–Trevisan [34]). A graph G is spectrally threshold dominated if, for every
k, some threshold graph of the same order and size majorizes G’s top-k Laplacian eigenvalue sum.
Every spectrally threshold dominated graph admits a threshold graph of the same order and size with
at least as large Laplacian energy. Split graphs are shown to be spectrally threshold dominated, as are
disjoint unions and complements of spectrally threshold dominated graphs. The authors conjecture
that every graph is spectrally threshold dominated, which (if true) would show that threshold graphs
maximize Laplacian energy among all graphs of given order and size, and that universal spectral
threshold dominance is equivalent to Brouwer’s conjecture for Laplacian eigenvalue sums.



                                                  8
   Within the class of threshold graphs itself, the extremal Laplacian-energy graphs have been
pinned down exactly:
Theorem 6.3 (Das–Mojallal [23]). The threshold graphs with the first four minimal Laplacian
energies, and those with the largest and second-largest Laplacian energies, are determined explicitly
in terms of n, m, and the trace of the degree sequence.
Theorem 6.4 (Helmberg–Trevisan [33]). Among connected threshold graphs on n vertices and m
edges, the maximum Laplacian energy is attained by one of two explicit extremal degree vectors, and
for threshold graphs of fixed order (edges free) the maximizer is the “pineapple” graph with trace
⌊2n/3⌋.
   A majorization-theoretic treatment computes the degree sequences of threshold graphs that
maximize or minimize Laplacian energy for fixed n and m, using Ferrers-diagram majorization
arguments, and constructs pairs of threshold graphs of the same order with equal Laplacian energy
but different Laplacian spectra.
Theorem 6.5 ([34]). A sharp eigenvalue-sum bound is used to give a full proof of the maximum
Laplacian energy conjecture for connected graphs, settling the connected case of the longstanding
conjecture that threshold graphs (specifically the pineapple graph) maximize Laplacian energy among
all connected graphs of given order and size — extending Theorem 6.2’s partial resolution.
Remark 6.6. This last result is very important and settles, for connected graphs, a conjecture that
had stood since Gutman and Zhou’s original introduction of Laplacian energy.

6.3    Normalized and other energy variants
The normalized adjacency energy (equal, it turns out, to the normalized Laplacian Randić index)
of threshold graphs is computed exactly in Section 10; distance, distance Laplacian, and distance
signless Laplacian energies are discussed alongside their respective spectra in Section 7.

7.2   The distance Laplacian spectrum
Since D(G)1 is not generally proportional to 1 unless G is transmission-regular, the distance matrix
itself does not inherit a clean closed form the way the Laplacian does. The distance Laplacian,
however, does — via a short master identity.
Proposition 7.3 (Master identity). For any graph G of diameter at most 2 on n vertices,
                                     DL (G) = 2nI − 2J − L(G).
Consequently spec(DL (G)) = {0} ∪ {2n − µ : µ ∈ spec(L(G)) \ {0}}, a reflection of the ordinary
Laplacian spectrum.

threshold graphs specifically, a fully explicit, combinatorial formula for the distance Laplacian spec-
trum — this combination is identified independently by Lather, Gupta, and Balaji [43] and by
Megha and Parvathy [51], both in 2025–2026.
Theorem 7.4 (Lather–Gupta–Balaji [43]). Let G be a connected threshold graph on n vertices with

  2. the dominating vertices of G can be identified from the eigenvectors of DL (G);
  3. among all connected threshold graphs on n vertices, every coefficient of the characteristic poly-

Theorem 7.5 (Megha–Parvathy [51]). Working directly with the equitable partition of the creation

Remark 7.6. These two results are complementary: Lather, Gupta, and Balaji’s Theorem 7.4 is
obtained via the general master identity plus the classical Merris theorem (and gives the sharp
cospectrality / extremal-coefficient statements), while Megha and Parvathy’s equitable-partition
route gives an independent, self-contained derivation well suited to classifying graphs with few

The eccentricity matrix ε(G) is obtained from the distance matrix D(G) by retaining, in each row
and each column, only the entries equal to the eccentricity of the corresponding vertex pair (the
largest distance achieved), and setting all other entries to zero; it is sometimes called the Dmax -
matrix, and can be viewed as a counterpart to the adjacency matrix (which instead retains only the
smallest nonzero distances).

the eccentricity eigenvalues are computed explicitly via a quotient matrix Bm constructed from b.

an eigenvalue-free interval around (−2, −1) is established.

9    The Seidel spectrum
The Seidel matrix S(G) = J − I − 2A(G) gives a ±1-valued encoding of adjacency, closely related
to two-graphs and switching classes. A system of lines L in the Euclidean d−dimensional space
Rd is said to be equiangular if all the lines meet at a point and have pairwise equal angle. The
constant angle is called the common angle of the system. There is a closed correspondence between
the equiangular line and (−1, 0, 1)−Seidel matrix. Seidel and Lint [46] first notice this important
relationship around 1966. Later Greaves et al. [28, 29, 30] develope several significant results in the
theory of equiangular lines in connection with Seidel matrix. In fact, Seidel matrix plays a key role
to study the structure of equiangular lines in space geometry.

Theorem 9.1 (Del-Vecchio–Abdón [25]). The number of distinct Seidel eigenvalues of a threshold
graph is determined directly from its binary representation, and families of Seidel-integral threshold
graphs (all Seidel eigenvalues integers) are constructed explicitly.

    Building on this, a more detailed structural and cospectrality analysis was carried out shortly
after:

Theorem 9.2 (Mandal–Mehatari [49]). For a connected threshold graph G, the Seidel characteristic
polynomial and det S(G) are computed explicitly from the creation sequence via the Seidel quotient
matrix. Closed formulas are given for the multiplicity of the (trivial) eigenvalues ±1. Threshold


                                                    13
graphs with at most five distinct Seidel eigenvalues are classified completely: G has exactly two dis-
tinct Seidel eigenvalues if and only if G is the complete graph Kn or the star K1,n−1 , and no threshold
graph has exactly three distinct Seidel eigenvalues. Finally, explicit pairs of non-isomorphic thresh-
old graphs that are Seidel-cospectral are constructed, showing threshold graphs are not determined
by their Seidel spectrum in general.

    The collection of all graphs that can be obtained from G by switching on every possible subset
of V is called the switching class of G. Two graphs G and H are called the switching equivalent
(or simply equivalent) if they belong to the same switching class. Otherwise, we call them simply
inequivalent. If two graphs are equivalent (res. inequivalent) then their Seidel matrices are said to
be equivalent (res. inequivalent). Equivalent Seidel matrices are cospectral, but starting from n = 8
examples of inequivalent cospectral Seidel matrices (of order n) appear. That means there does not
exist any cospectral graphs (which are not switching equivalent) of order n ≤ 7. Szöllösi et al. [61]
tabulated the number of inequivalent cospectral Seidel matrices of order n where n ≤ 13. Although
Mandal et al. [49] find the cospectral pairs, but they belong to the same switching class.
In spirit of these works, Xiong et al. derive an open interval that contains no Seidel eigenvalue
(non-trivial) of a threshold graph.

10     The normalized adjacency and normalized Laplacian spectrum
The normalized adjacency matrix A(G) = Deg(G)−1/2 A(G) Deg(G)−1/2 is related to the normalized
(random-walk) Laplacian by L(G) = I − A(G); its eigenvalues are the normalized eigenvalues of G.

Theorem 10.1 (Banerjee–Mehatari [16]). The normalized eigenvalues and normalized adjacency
energy of a connected threshold graph are computed from its normalized equitable-partition quotient
matrix. The normalized adjacency energy is shown to equal the normalized Laplacian (Randić)
energy of the graph. Threshold graphs with at most five distinct normalized eigenvalues are charac-
terized explicitly.

   This result builds on the foundational normalized-Laplacian spectral theory of Banerjee and
Jost [17], specializing their general machinery (originally developed to study spectral signatures of
network motifs) to the threshold-graph setting.


11     Extremal spectral radius problems
Threshold graphs arise not only as objects of direct spectral study but as the extremal graphs in
a wide range of spectral optimization problems over graphs of fixed order and/or size — a role
traceable to Brualdi and Hoffman’s early observation that extremal (0, 1)-matrices for the spectral
radius take a stepwise form coinciding with the threshold-graph adjacency structure.

Theorem 11.1 (Brualdi-Hoffman [20]). Among all connected graphs with a given number of vertices
n and edges m, the graph maximizing the adjacency spectral radius is always a threshold graph. An
independent proof shows that a connected extremal graph can contain none of P4 , C4 , or 2K2 as an
induced subgraph, which is precisely the threshold-graph characterization.



                                                  14
    Despite this characterization being known in principle, identifying which threshold graph is
extremal for given (n, m) remains theoretically difficult in general.

Theorem 11.2 (Radanović–Fellague–Ostojić–Stevanović–Davidović [55]). Metaheuristic search meth-
ods (incomplete enumeration guided by structural properties of threshold graphs) are developed to
find threshold graphs of maximum spectral radius for given (n, m), complementing the sparse set of
cases with a full theoretical resolution.

Theorem 11.3 (Csikvári–Damnjanović–Stevanović–Wagner [22]). Two-sided bounds on the adja-
cency spectral radius of a connected threshold graph are given, expressed as the largest roots of two
cubic polynomials whose coefficients are read off from the creation sequence.

    The role of threshold graphs as extremal cases extends well beyond the adjacency spectral ra-
dius: they appear as the extremal graphs (or spanning supergraphs of the extremal graphs) in
spectral-radius sufficient conditions for graph-factor existence, Hamiltonicity, and fractional match-
ing number, across the adjacency, signless Laplacian, distance, distance Laplacian, and distance
signless Laplacian spectral radii alike — see Section 7.3 for a distance signless Laplacian instance,
and the signless Brouwer conjecture (Theorem 5.1) for a signless Laplacian instance.

13     Integral threshold graphs
A graph matrix M (G) is integral if every eigenvalue of M (G) is an integer. The origin of integral
graph goes back to the foundational work of Harary et al. [32]. We close the matrix-by-matrix
survey with the study of integrality, synthesizing and extending the classification results scattered
through Sections 3–10. As in Section 12, we found no existing paper treating this question uniformly
across all seven matrices, so this section presents original analysis alongside the surveyed literature.
    The starting observation, verified directly for every matrix below, is that local (twin-class)
eigenvalues are always integers — they are read off directly from transmissions, degrees, or fixed
small constants, all of which are integer-valued by construction (Section 1). Consequently, for every
matrix in this section, integrality of M (G) is governed entirely by the quotient matrix of Section 1’s
equitable partition, exactly as multiplicity and cospectrality questions were throughout this survey.

13.1    Two matrices are integral unconditionally
Theorem 13.1. Every connected threshold graph is L-integral.

Proof. Immediate from Theorem 4.1: spec(L(G)) is the conjugate partition of the (integer) degree
sequence, hence integer-valued.

Theorem 13.2. Every connected threshold graph is DL -integral.

Proof. By Proposition 7.3, spec(DL (G)) = {0} ∪ {2n − µ : µ ∈ spec(L(G)) \ {0}}. Since n ∈ Z
and, by Theorem 13.1, every µ ∈ spec(L(G)) is an integer, every element of spec(DL (G)) is an
integer.

   Theorem 13.2 is, to our knowledge, a new observation: it does not appear explicitly in [45, 51],
though it follows immediately from results already in both.

Proposition 13.3. Unlike L(G), the ordinary signless Laplacian Q(G) = Deg(G) + A(G) of a
threshold graph does not in general have an integer (or otherwise simply-described closed-form)
spectrum.

    This is not a theorem in the traditional sense but an observation confirmed by direct compu-
tation: e.g. the creation sequence (0, 1, 0, 1) gives spec(Q(G)) ≈ {0.511, 1, 2, 2.711, 5.779}. This
traces to the structural fact: Q(G) lacks the L(G)1 = 0 property that makes threshold-graph
Laplacian spectra reduce to a combinatorial object.

threshold graphs within the cograph hierarchy. As noted in Section 5, the main-Q-eigenvalue char-
acterization of threshold graphs [64] has been extended to this broader class [41, 42], and represents
one of the most active current directions for lifting threshold-graph spectral results to a wider
setting.

14.2    Threshold hypergraphs
A hypergraph is a generalization of a graph in which an edge can connect any number of vertices
rather than exactly two vertices. Formally, a hypergraph H = (V, E) consists of a non-empty set V of
vertices and a family E of non-empty subsets of V , called hyperedges. Each hyperedge may contain
two or more vertices, representing relationships among multiple objects simultaneously. If every
hyperedge contains exactly two vertices, the hypergraph reduces to an ordinary graph. Hypergraphs
are classified into different types, such as uniform hypergraphs, in which all hyperedges have the
same cardinality, and simple hypergraphs, in which no hyperedge is repeated. Thus, hypergraph
theory provides a powerful mathematical framework for modelling complex relationships that cannot
be adequately represented by ordinary graphs. The study of matrix associated with the hypergraph
is known as Spectral Hypergraph Theory. It opens up a new directions for future research (see
[13, 14, 15]). The following two theorems address the hypergraph-theoretic aspects of threshold
graphs.

Theorem 14.1 (Banerjee–Mishra–Parui [18]). A threshold hypergraph is defined via a generalized
binary-string construction, and its Laplacian spectrum is computed from the string representation.
The Laplacian spectrum of certain threshold hypergraphs is shown to be r-integral (an integer mul-
tiple of some fixed rational r) and is computed explicitly from the Ferrer’s diagram of the degree
sequence — a direct hypergraph analogue of Merris’s Theorem 4.1.

Theorem 14.2 (Abdón et al.[1]). This article introduces a definition of k-uniform threshold hyper-
graph through a binary sequence, a natural extension of the classical definition for threshold graphs.


                                                 19
It characterizes some of its eigenvalues and multiplicities by means of combinatorial numbers, de-
rived from edge counts. An important problem addressed in Spectral Graph Theory is to find graphs
with few distinct eigenvalues. This allows to construct k-uniform threshold hypergraphs having an
arbitrary number of vertices with few distinct eigenvalues.

14.3    Chain graphs: the bipartite analogue
Chain graphs (equivalently {C3 , C5 , 2K2 }-free bipartite graphs, or double nested graphs) are the
natural bipartite counterpart of threshold graphs, built by an analogous (but not identical) binary
creation sequence, and are the extremal graphs for adjacency spectral radius among bipartite graphs
of given order and size, exactly as threshold graphs are extremal among all graphs. Their adjacency
eigenvalue location [3], Laplacian spectrum [5], Seidel spectrum and energy [50], eccentricity spectra
[67], eccentricity inertia indices [37], energy and Laplacian energy [24] – each have been studied in
close parallel to the threshold-graph results surveyed above, and eigenvalue-free intervals for the
distance-based matrices of both threshold and chain graphs are treated together in [4]. Notably,
connected chain graphs do not generally have diameter at most 2 (diameter 3 is typical once the
creation sequence has more than one alternation), so the clean master identity of Proposition 7.3
is unavailable for chain graphs’ distance Laplacian, and the distance-type spectral theory of chain
graphs must be — and has been — built by more direct means.

14.4    Random threshold graphs
A random threshold graph is a random graph generated by a stochastic process in which vertices are
added one at a time, each being designated as either an isolated vertex or a dominating vertex. In the
random model, the type of each newly added vertex is determined probabilistically, often according
to a fixed probability p of adding a dominating vertex and (1 − p) of adding an isolated vertex.
Thus, the resulting graph depends on a random binary sequence encoding the construction process.
Random threshold graphs form an important class of random graphs because they combine a simple
probabilistic construction with a highly structured graph-theoretic characterization. As noted in
Section 3, threshold graphs generated by a random (e.g. Pólya urn) process rather than a fixed
creation sequence have been studied, with exact finite-n degree distributions and asymptotic (n →
∞) eigenvalue distribution results for both the adjacency and Laplacian matrices, plus an explicit
closed-form Laplacian eigenbasis in the random setting. In 2008, Reilly et al. [58] proposed two
equivalent models for random threshold graphs and investigated the properties including chromatic
number, algebraic connectivity, Hamiltonian cycles, and perfect matchings. Recently, in 2026, Yu
et al. [68] introduces a random threshold graph model based on a two-colour Pólya urn process; it
studies degree distributions, Laplacian matrices and several spectral properties.


15     Open problems
We collect here open problems drawn directly from the surveyed literature, together with one
problem (Open Problem 15.6) suggested by the comparative view of Table 1.
Open Problem 15.1 (Distance signless Laplacian of threshold graphs). Give an explicit, closed-
form distance signless Laplacian spectrum for threshold graphs directly from the creation sequence
(analogous to Theorem 7.4 for the distance Laplacian), and determine whether threshold graphs
are determined by their distance signless Laplacian spectrum.




                                                 20
Open Problem 15.2 (A distinguished graph for cographs). Aguilar, Ficarra, Schurman, and Sullivan
[2] ask whether, within the broader class of cographs, there is a distinguished graph (or small family
of graphs) playing the organizing role that the anti-regular graph Hn plays for threshold graphs.

Conjecture 15.3 (Helmberg–Trevisan [34]). Every graph is spectrally threshold dominated (The-
orem 6.2): for every graph G and every k, some threshold graph of the same order and size as G
majorizes G’s top-k Laplacian eigenvalue sum.

Open Problem 15.4 (Universal spectral threshold dominance). Conjecture 15.3, if true, would show
that threshold graphs maximize Laplacian energy among all graphs of given order and size, and
would be equivalent to Brouwer’s conjecture for Laplacian eigenvalue sums. It is verified for split
graphs and for disjoint unions/complements of spectrally threshold dominated graphs, but remains
open in general.
Open Problem 15.5 (Main eigenvalues of quasi-threshold graphs, k ≥ 3). The characterization of
quasi-threshold graphs with k main signless Laplacian eigenvalues (Section 5) is complete for k = 2
but only partial for k = 3 [42]; a full characterization for general k remains open.
Open Problem 15.6 (Unifying spectral determination). Table 1 shows that threshold graphs are
determined by their adjacency, Laplacian, and distance Laplacian spectra, but not by their distance
or Seidel spectra. Is there a structural criterion on the matrix M (e.g. some algebraic relationship
to J, or to the twin-class/quotient-matrix reduction of Section 1) that predicts, without a separate
ad hoc proof for each case, whether M determines threshold graphs up to isomorphism?
Open Problem 15.7 (Eccentricity-matrix cospectrality). Whether threshold graphs are determined
by their eccentricity spectrum does not appear to have been settled in the literature surveyed here,
despite the detailed eigenvalue-location results of Theorems 8.1–8.2.
Open Problem 15.8 (Extremal threshold graphs for fixed (n, m)). While it is known that the
adjacency-spectral-radius maximizer among connected graphs of order n and size m is always a
threshold graph, no general closed-form description of which threshold graph is extremal is known;
current progress is via metaheuristic search [55] rather than closed-form characterization.
Open Problem 15.9 (Integral threshold graphs beyond the complete split graph). Section 13 char-
acterizes A-, S-, D-, DQ -, and ε-integrality completely for the two-block family CS(s, t), but the
general (m ≥ 2) case remains open for every one of these five matrices. Even the next simplest
case, m = 2 (four blocks), is uncharacterized: is there a clean Diophantine condition analogous to
Theorem 13.7, or — as the block count grows — does the governing polynomial’s degree grow with
m in a way that forecloses a uniform closed-form answer?


16    Conclusion
Threshold graphs occupy a genuinely unusual position in spectral graph theory: a single combina-
torial object — the binary creation sequence — drives exact or near-exact spectral computations
across essentially every matrix the field has studied, from the classical adjacency and Laplacian
matrices through the modern distance-based and eccentricity matrices. The common machinery
throughout is the equitable partition induced by twin vertices, reducing an n-dimensional spectral
problem to a quotient matrix of size proportional to the number of alternations in the creation
sequence — together with, for several matrices, a master identity connecting the matrix of inter-
est to a better-understood relative (most notably the distance-Laplacian-to-Laplacian identity of
Proposition 7.3).


                                                 21
     The literature has matured furthest for the adjacency matrix (essentially complete: eigenvalue
location, cospectrality, energy, and eigenvalue-free intervals are all settled) and the ordinary and
distance Laplacians (both admit exact closed forms via Merris’s theorem and its master-identity
consequence). It is comparatively less developed for the distance signless Laplacian (Open Prob-
lem 15.1) and for several natural generalizations — quasi-threshold graphs, weighted threshold
graphs, and threshold hypergraphs — each the subject of active work as recently as 2024 and 2025.
The cross-cutting integrality study of Section 13 reinforces this same divide from a new angle: L
and DL are unconditionally integral for every threshold graph via the master identity, while A,
S, D, DQ , and ε each require a genuine Diophantine condition, fully resolved here only for the
simplest (m = 1) family. We expect the open problems collected in Section 15, together with the
still-developing extremal spectral radius literature of Section 11 and the integrality questions of
Section 13, to remain fruitful directions for the next several years of research on this exceptionally
well-behaved graph class.
