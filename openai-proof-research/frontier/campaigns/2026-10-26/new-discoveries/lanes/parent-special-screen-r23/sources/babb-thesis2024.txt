Abstract

Fourier expansions are a key tool in the analysis of partial di!erential equations.
Irregular functions can still be expanded as Fourier series, and then by truncating
the Fourier series one obtains a sequence of smooth functions that converges to the
original irregular function. This idea—and related techniques—forms a key part of
many proofs in the area. However, truncated Fourier series—and, more generally,
truncated eigenfunction expansions—may not converge in Lp for p ↑= 2, and this can
prove problematic in many situations. Based on the observation that carefully chosen
truncations of Fourier series do converge in Lp when p ↑= 2, we have investigated
how much this can be carried over the eigenfunction expansions in other domains
(the Fourier series case corresponding to the Laplacian in a square domain).
       We have obtained new convergence results for certain types of triangular
domains, as well as divergence results for 45-90-45 triangles.
       We have analysed the situation in the disc, where the eigenfunctions are
products of trigonometric and Bessel functions, giving rise to Bessel–Fourier series.
We have constructed integrable functions whose Bessel–Fourier coe"cients decay
slower than m→ω for 0 ↭ ω < 1/2, subject to the condition xω+1 f (x) ↓ L1 [0, 1]. We
provide numerical evidence to suggest that the same should hold when ω = 1/2, and
show how such a result could be used to construct an integrable function on the disc
whose Bessel–Fourier series diverges for any truncation method.
       Finally, we consider the general setting in which we perturb a planar domain,

       After a review of the classic theory of Fourier series (Chapter 2), we will
begin (in Chapter 3) by investigating triangular domains. Following early work of
Lamé (1833), other authors such as Práger (1998) and McCartin (2003) have derived
explicit trigonometric expressions for Dirichlet eigenfunctions on the equilateral tri-
angle. Neither of these authors, however, consider questions of convergence for the
associated eigenfunction series, and subsequent work by Adcock (2009), Huybrechs
et al. (2011), and Sun and Li (2005), restricts attention to numerical methods or
convergence in L2 -based spaces such as H k . To our knowledge, this is the first
treatment of the Lp convergence of series of eigenfunctions on the triangle for p ↑= 2.
       We will next turn our attention to the disc in Chapters 4 and 5. The eigen-
functions for the disc are products of trigonometric and Bessel functions and so
share a similar product structure to the classic Fourier series on T2 . Specifically, the
eigenfunctions are of the form

In this chapter we establish Lp convergence for trigonometric series of eigenfunctions
for the Dirichlet Laplacian on three types of triangular domains: (i) the 45-90-45
triangle, (ii) the equilateral triangle, and (iii) the hemiequilateral triangle (i.e. half
an equilateral triangle cut along its height).
       Eigenfunctions for the latter types, (ii) and (iii), were obtained by Práger
(1998) and McCartin (2003), as mentioned in Chapter 1. Using their insights, we
prove that, on each of these domains, any Lp function can be written as a norm-
convergent series of eigenfunctions. We also give a complete treatment of these issues
for the simpler case (i). As far as we know, the result for type-(i) triangles is new.
       In Section 3.1 we derive the eigenfunctions for the type-(i) triangles, prove
their completeness, and establish Lp convergence of the associated series. This serves
as a useful prototype for the arguments that we will develop in the following sections.
       The result for equilateral domains is obtained by a decomposition of a func-
tion into a symmetric and antisymmetric part with respect to the line x = 0. This
reduces the problem to type-(iii) domains, which, once solved, easily yields the equi-
lateral case.
       Thus, in Sections 3.2 and 3.4 we separately derive the antisymmetric and
symmetric modes, following the ingenious approach of Práger (1998) (see Fig. 3.2).

3.7       Concluding remarks
Our argument made use of Práger’s cunning triangle-to-rectangle transformation in
order to reduce the convergence problem on the triangle to the well-known conver-
gence on the rectangle. In a similar, but more direct way, we were able to obtain
these results for the 45-90-45 triangle.

following theorem due to Lamé (1833) and reported as Theorem 3.1 in McCartin

3.8      Appendix: Non-convergence for spherical cuto"s
Let us return to the Fourier series on the 45-90-45 triangle T from Section 3.1. There,
we ordered the eigenfunctions um,n by the condition 0 < m < n and summed in the
range n ↭ N . As we remarked in the introduction, a more natural choice would
be to sum over all um,n with eigenvalue ϱm,n ↭ N . Since ϱm,n = ϖ(m2 + n2 ), this

        Given a counterexample f on the square, we would need to find a function

6.6      Conclusions and open problems
Our approach has the following important limitation: the projections PF map onto
eigenspaces which do not split eigenvalues. Eigenvalue splitting is not allowed be-
cause of Kato’s projection formula, as discussed in the remark immediately after
Theorem 6.1.1.
         The following example motivates our interest in relaxing the notion of split-
ting.

Example 6.6.1. Let ! = [0, 1]2 , a unit square; then the eigenvalues of the Dirichlet
Laplacian are ϖ 2 (m2 +n2 ) for (m, n) running over N2 . Fix any N ↓ N. The following
results are classic (see Grafakos, 2014b):

  (i) If we take FN = {(m, n) : 0 < m, n ↭ N }, then PFN is the partial sum operator
        corresponding to ‘cubic cuto!’ truncations of a double sine-series on the torus.
        For any value of 1 < p < ↗, these projections are uniformly bounded in N ,
        which is equivalent to saying that the partial sums PFN u converge to u for any
        u ↓ Lp .

 (ii) If we take BN = {(m, n) : m2 + n2 ↭ N 2 }, then PBN is the partial sum
        operator corresponding to ‘spherical cuto!’ truncations of a double sine-series
        on the torus. These partial sums do not converge in any Lp space other than
        p = 2.

         It would be interesting to transfer the convergence results for (i) to other
domains obtained by perturbing the square by some suitable ↼, as described here.
However, by definition, the sets BN will not split eigenvalues, while the sets FN
will often split eigenvalues. Take, for instance, the pairs (5, 5) and (1, 7), both
corresponding to the eigenvalue 50ϖ 2 . Then (5, 5) ↓ F6 but (1, 7) ↓
                                                                    / F6 .
         Denote this ‘bad region’ by $N , i.e.

             $N = {(m, n) ↓ FN : m2 + n2 = k 2 + l2 for some (k, l) ↓
                                                                    / FN }.

Then FN splits eigenvalues if, and only, if $N ↑= ⫋ (see Fig. 6.1).

         It is rather surprising that this ‘small’ region $N lying between the circle
and the square is responsible for such dramatic di!erences in the convergence of the
partial sums. Indeed, Fe!erman’s proof shows that curvature is a key part of the
failure of Lp convergence.

Conclusion

                                    ‘Begin at the beginning,’ the King said, very
                                    gravely, ‘and go on till you come to the end: then
                                    stop.’
                                                                         —Lewis Carroll

We have certainly come to the end of this thesis, but will disobey the King’s injunc-
tion to stop, for there remain many open questions to stimulate further research.
       We have looked closer at the issue of convergence for some of the most natural
domains and began the study of a more general procedure of perturbation of domains.
       Our analysis in Chapter 3 shows that, for those triangles amenable to the
method of reflection—viz. the 45-90-45 isosceles, the hemi-equilateral and the equi-
lateral triangles—Lp convergence results carry over cleanly from the square for cubic
truncations of the partial sums. We even saw that Fe!erman’s proof of the Ball Mul-
tiplier Theorem can be adapted to yield a similar negative result for p ↑= 2 on the
45-90-45 triangle.
       Lamé’s theorem, on the other hand, raises a more serious and unsettling issue:
for other triangles, we cannot expect to have trigonometric eigenfunctions at all!
       Thus, the open questions that remain are

  (i) Can we show that spherical truncations diverge for the equilateral triangle?

 (ii) Can we find some truncation method that yields Lp convergence for other
     types of triangular domains?

       The next natural candidate was the disc, whose eigenfunctions are the ‘nearly
trigonometric’ Bessel functions. We were unable to establish Lp convergence results
on the disc, but were lead to a very interesting investigation of the decay rates of
