                                                of the first and second kinds. In particular, we derive new results using elemen-

   The somewhat rich and unexpected results are perhaps surprisingly easy to

relations algorithm PSLQ, the Inverse Symbolic Calculator (ISC, now hosted at

established by the following elementary techniques:
    (1) Connections with and transforms of hypergeometric and Meijer G-functions
        ([16]), as in the case of random walk integrals (Section 4).
    (2) Interchange order of summation and integration (Section 3).
    (3) Evaluate a closed form for the primitive (Section 5).
    (4) Change the variable x to x′ (Section 5).
    (5) Change the variable followed by quadratic transforms (Section 5).
    (6) Use a Fourier series (Section 6).
    (7) Apply Legendre’s relation (Section 7).
    (8) Differentiate a product of elliptic integrals and integrate by parts (Section
        8).

Remark 2. We sketch another proof by expanding (27) into partial fractions.
   As each partial fraction has at most a 4th power on the denominator, the sum
can involve at most ζ(2), ζ(3), ζ(4), and some contribution from the linear denom-
inators.
   Because the linear denominator terms must converge, their sum must eventually
telescope, and hence contribute only a rational number.
   We recall that partial fractions can be obtained via a derivative process akin to
computing Taylor series coefficients; indeed, if we write
                   f (x)       A1       A2              An
                         n
                           =        +        2
                                               +···+          ,
                 (x − a)     (x − a) (x − a)         (x − a)n
then An = f (a), An−1 = f ′ (a)/1!, . . . , A1 = f (n−1) (a)/(n − 1)!.
   When applied to (27), it is easy to check that, when n ≡ 3 mod 4, the presence of
the numerator 2k+m+1 makes the terms with quadratic and quartic denominators
telescope out, leaving us with rational numbers (these terms occur in pairs related
by the transformation k 7→ (−n − 1)/2 − k, where said linear numerator switches
sign). Similarly, the terms with cubic denominators double.
   When n ≡ 1 mod 4, 2k + m + 1 cancels out with one of the denominators,
making it a cubic; it can be similarly checked that its partial fraction can have no
quadratic term: this is equivalent to showing (27) with all powers of 2k + m + 1
removed has 0 derivative at k = (−n − 1)/4, which is true because it is symmetric
around that point.
   Therefore only the cubic terms remain, giving us ζ(3).
   This type of partial fraction argument is at the heart of the result that infinitely
many odd zeta values are irrational (see [5], which, incidentally, is the motivation
for Zudilin’s Theorem).
