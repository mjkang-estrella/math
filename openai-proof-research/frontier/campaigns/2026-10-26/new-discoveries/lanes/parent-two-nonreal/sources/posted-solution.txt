### A stronger localization theorem

Let

$$
A(x)=ax+b,
\qquad
B(x)=cx+d,
\qquad
a,b,d<0<c,
$$

and define

$$
W_0(x)=1,
\qquad
W_1(x)=x,
\qquad
W_n(x)=A(x)W_{n-1}(x)+B(x)W_{n-2}(x)
\quad(n\geq2).
$$

In fact, for every $n\geq2$, the polynomial $W_n$ has at least
$n-2$ **distinct** real zeros in

$$
\left(-\infty,-\frac dc\right).
\tag{1}
$$

Because $\deg W_n=n$, it follows that $W_n$ has at most two nonreal
zeros. This proves the conjecture, including its full range of real
parameters.

### A symmetric tridiagonal representation

Put

$$
\xi=-\frac dc>0.
$$

For $x<\xi$, define

$$
t(x)=\sqrt{-B(x)}=\sqrt{-cx-d}>0
$$

and consider the real symmetric $n\times n$ matrix

$$
J_n(x)=
\begin{pmatrix}
x&t(x)&0&\cdots&0\\
t(x)&A(x)&t(x)&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&t(x)&A(x)&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&t(x)\\
0&\cdots&0&t(x)&A(x)
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{2}
$$

Write $D_k(x)$ for the determinant of its leading $k\times k$ principal
submatrix and set $D_0(x)=1$. Expansion along the last row gives

$$
D_1(x)=x,
\qquad
D_k(x)=A(x)D_{k-1}(x)-t(x)^2D_{k-2}(x)
\quad(k\geq2).
$$

Since $-t(x)^2=B(x)$, these are exactly the defining initial conditions
and recurrence for $W_k$. Therefore,

$$
\boxed{\det J_n(x)=W_n(x)}
\qquad(x<\xi).
\tag{3}
$$

### Endpoint inertia

Let $\nu_+(M)$ denote the number of strictly positive eigenvalues of a
real symmetric matrix $M$, counted with multiplicity. The matrix in (2)
extends continuously to $x=\xi$, where $t(\xi)=0$. Since

$$
\xi>0,
\qquad
A(\xi)=a\xi+b<0,
$$

we have

$$
J_n(\xi)
=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\xi,A(\xi),\ldots,A(\xi)\bigr),
\qquad
\nu_+\bigl(J_n(\xi)\bigr)=1.
\tag{4}
$$

At the other end, set $x=-R$ and let $R\to\infty$. Then

$$
x=-R,
\qquad
A(-R)=(-a)R+b,
\qquad
t(-R)=\sqrt{cR-d}.
$$

Because $-a>0$, for every sufficiently large $R$,

$$
-R+\sqrt{cR-d}<0,
\qquad
(-a)R+b-2\sqrt{cR-d}>0.
\tag{5}
$$

The Gershgorin interval corresponding to the first row of $J_n(-R)$ lies
entirely in $( -\infty,0)$, whereas all the other Gershgorin intervals lie
entirely in $(0,\infty)$. Since these two collections are disjoint, their
eigenvalue counts equal the numbers of corresponding rows. Consequently,

$$
\nu_+\bigl(J_n(-R)\bigr)=n-1
\tag{6}
$$

for all sufficiently large $R$.

### Counting the crossings

For every $x<\xi$, all off-diagonal entries of $J_n(x)$ are strictly
positive. Hence

$$
\dim\ker J_n(x)\leq1.
\tag{7}
$$

Indeed, the first component of a vector in the kernel determines its second
component from the first row, and each subsequent row determines the next
component uniquely. If the first component is zero, this recursion forces
every component to vanish.

The eigenvalues of $J_n(x)$ vary continuously with $x$. Its positive
inertia can change only when $J_n(x)$ is singular, which by (3) is
equivalent to

$$
W_n(x)=0.
$$

At any such point, (7) shows that at most one eigenvalue can cross zero.
Therefore each distinct real zero in $(-R,\xi)$ changes $\nu_+$ by at
most one. Equations (4) and (6) give the total change

$$
\left|
\nu_+\bigl(J_n(-R)\bigr)
-\nu_+\bigl(J_n(\xi)\bigr)
\right|
=n-2.
$$

Thus $W_n$ has at least $n-2$ distinct real zeros in $(-R,\xi)$, and
therefore in the interval (1).

Finally, the recurrence shows that

$$
\deg W_n=n,
\qquad
[x^n]W_n(x)=a^{n-1}\neq0
\quad(n\geq1).
$$

At most two zeros can remain outside the $n-2$ distinct real zeros already
located. Since $W_n$ has real coefficients, any nonreal zeros form one
conjugate pair. Hence every $W_n$ has at most two nonreal zeros, as
asserted.

[D. G. L. Wang and Jiarui Zhang, *Piecewise interlacing zeros of polynomials*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.04225), Conjecture 5.2.
