                                           The goal of this article is to introduce and study a new natural q-analogue of the Pythagoras equation.

                                           where C ∗ is the polynomial reciprocal to C, i.e.

                                                                                       C ∗ (q) = q deg(C) C(q −1 ).                                    (3)

of polynomials and Hilbert’s seventeenth problem. However, polynomials satisfying this equation cannot
have nice properties we will be interested in.


2     An interesting class of solutions
We will search for solutions to the equation (2) satisfying the following properties which seem quite
natural from a combinatorial point of view.
    1. The polynomials A, B, C have positive integer coefficients;
    2. The polynomials A and B are self-reciprocal, or “palindromic”;
    3. The polynomials A, B, C and C ∗ are monic, i.e. their leading and lower degree coefficients are equal
       to 1.
    The positivity condition 1 implies that the triple of integers
                                        (a, b, c) := (A(1), B(1), C(1))
is a classical Pythagorean triple. We will therefore say that the triplet of polynomials (A, B, C) cor-
responds to this Pythagorean triple and is its q-analogue, or “quantization”. This term and notion is
extensively used in mathematical physics, but also in combinatorics. Roughly speaking, quantization
means replacing a single quantity by a discrete (finite) sequence that potentially contain more informa-
tion. In our situation, we replace single integers a, b, c by sequences of coefficients of the polynomials
A, B, C, and each coefficient of these polynomials must have a meaning. Another approach to quantization
consists in replacing commutative algebraic structure by non-commutative, as explored in [1]. Note that
two approaches are related, but their comparison is far beyond the scope of this article.
    Our main result is the following existence statement.


                                                         2
Theorem 1. For every standard Pythagorean triple (a, b, c) there exists a solution (A, B, C) to (2) satis-
fying the conditions 1, 2, and 3 and corresponding to (a, b, c).
    To illustrate this theorem, let us give an infinite series of solutions enumerated by integers:

                                        A n1 (q)      =   (1 + q n ) [n]q ,
                                        B n1 (q)      =   [n + 1]q [n − 1]q ,
                                                                      2
                                         C n1 (q)     =   1 + q [n]q ,

where [n]q is the q-integer; see (4).
    Our construction of polynomial Pythagorean triples is based on the q-deformed action of the modular
group PSL(2, Z) on the rational projective line. It was used in [7] to define the notion of q-deformed
rational numbers. For more details, see [6] and a survey [9]. Note that our approach is quite close to that
of [4] where q-deformations of Markov triples were studied with the help of the q-deformed action of the
modular group.
    Similarly to the classical case, we obtain an infinite series of q-deformed Pythagorean triples organized
in a form of a binary tree. Replacing the rational numbers with q-rationals, we obtain a q-analogue of
the Euclid formula.
    We conjecture that our solutions have another remarkable property. A sequence of real numbers is said
to be unimodal if it increases (not strictly monotonically) to a maximum, then decreases monotonically.
Unimodal sequences have a single peak and no oscillations.
    We wish the following additional property
 4∗ . The sequences of coefficients of the polynomials A, B, C are unimodal,
but we are unable to guarantee it! The unimodality property is usually difficult to prove. Note that this
property for q-deformed rational numbers was conjectured in [7] and proved in [10].
Conjecture. The sequences of coefficients of the polynomials A, B, C constructed in this article are uni-
modal.
    Let us mention that the solutions to (2) that we construct are far from being the only existing
solutions. We will show that there are solutions to (2) satisfying the conditions 1, 2, 3, and the unimodality
property different from ours. Their classification is a challenging problem. It would also be interesting
to understand what distinguishes the class of solutions related to q-rationals.
