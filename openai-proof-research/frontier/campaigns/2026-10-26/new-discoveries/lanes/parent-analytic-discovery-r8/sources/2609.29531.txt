In particular κr vanishes identically on the diagonal A = B, which contains the ultras-
pherical case α = β: the Legendre and Gegenbauer cases need no phase constants. The
nontrivial content of (3) is therefore transverse to the diagonal. On the diagonal itself
the phase constants vanish but the series from which they are extracted (the amplitude,
introduced below) does not; it is a nontrivial asymptotic series. Our first question is
whether this series can be computed in closed form. The second feature is arithmetic:
the denominators 4 = 22 , 96 = 25 · 3, 7680 = 29 · 15 and, for κ4 , 86016 = 212 · 21 grow
mainly through powers of 2. In [17] it is showed that the power of 2 in the denomina-
tor of κr is at most 2Er with Er = 3r − 1 + ν2 ((r − 1)!), where ν2 denotes the 2-adic
valuation. Our second question is whether this bound is attained for every r. Both ques-
tions are answered in this paper: the diagonal series is the Stirling series of a quotient
of Gamma functions (Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.2), and the bound 2Er is sharp for
every r (Theorem 1.5). The proof of the second statement uses the first: the closed form
on the diagonal is the background over which the first-order variation transverse to the
diagonal is computed.
   Large-degree asymptotics for Jacobi polynomials and functions have a long history,
from the classical trigonometric and uniform expansions of Hahn [9] and Frenzen–
Wong [10] to Riemann–Hilbert treatments of Jacobi-type weights [12], fast computation
of Gauss–Jacobi nodes and weights [11, 14], nonoscillatory phase-function methods
[13], explicit error bounds [16] and, closest to our starting point, the inverse-factorial
large-degree expansions of Nemes [15] for Jacobi and related functions with fixed pa-
rameters, which carry explicit computable error bounds, are built from the same Hankel
coefficients as (6) below, and terminate at half-odd-integer parameters. These works
develop the expansions, their error bounds and the associated algorithms. The present
paper addresses a different question: the exact algebraic and arithmetic structure of the
coefficients of the phase expansion, and in particular of the constants κr .

   The proof of Theorem 1.6 proceeds as follows. The amplitude (6) is a Kampé de Fériet
double series (Lemma 7.2), in fact an Appell F3 function evaluated on P   a curve on which
it reduces to a single Gauss function (Theorem 7.3). Consequently n gn Z −n is the
expansion of a second parameter derivative of a zero-balanced 2 F1 at the boundary value
2+i0 (Corollary 7.4). The classical connection formula splits this boundary value into two
blocks which are quotients of Gamma functions at the Legendre point (Proposition 7.7);
the variation of one of them satisfies a first-order difference equation which is solved by
quadratures (Theorem 7.9); and the passage from the resulting convergent expressions
to the formal series in Z −1 is made by an asymptotic matching along a vertical line
(Subsection 7.6), whose estimates are given in Appendix A. Theorem 1.5 then follows from
the 2-adic analysis of the explicit series Q
                                           bΓ (Subsection 7.7): the exponent of the Gamma
quotient has Genocchi-number coefficients, and in the expansion of its exponential exactly
one term has minimal valuation.
   Besides these results the paper contains further exact structure of the amplitude. The
recurrences for Bessel polynomials used in the proof of Theorem 1.1 do not require
the special relation p2 + q 2 = 0 satisfied at the midpoint (Theorem 4.1); on the whole
curve described by the weights p, q as θ varies, where p + q = pq, the half-odd-integer
amplitudes satisfy a three-term contiguity whose numerators are given in closed form
in terms of ballot numbers (Theorems 5.2 and 5.4), and the derivatives of all orders
normal to that curve are determined recursively (Theorem 6.4). For independent half-
odd-integer parameters α, β the amplitudes satisfy a similar recurrence off the diagonal
(Theorem 7.1). The amplitude is an explicit sum of products of pairs of Gauss functions,
the r-th term being of order Z −3r (Corollary 8.2); from this P   expansion we derive four
valuation formulas for the numerator and the denominator of n gn Z −n (Theorem 10.3
and Proposition 10.4). Section 11 illustrates the use of (3) for the numerical computation
of zeros.
   Two statements of the paper are not proved in full generality, and neither is used
in the proofs of Theorems 1.1, 1.5 and 1.6. The first is the agreement of (7) with the
constants Ψr (π/2) of (3), which is proved for r ≤ 8 (Proposition 7.37); for r > 8 the
last assertion of Theorem 1.5 is a statement about the polynomials defined byP(7). The
second is a periodicity hypothesis under which the function whose expansion is n gn Z −n
is evaluated in closed form on the real axis (Proposition 7.28).
   Throughout the paper, identities in Z −1 (equivalently in v = Z −1 or in s−1 , s =
(1 − Z)/2) are identities of formal Laurent series unless convergence is explicitly asserted.
When a meromorphic function is introduced, its expansion for large Z is always under-
stood in the stated sector. For half-odd-integer parameters the identities are identities
of rational functions or of quotients of Gamma functions.
