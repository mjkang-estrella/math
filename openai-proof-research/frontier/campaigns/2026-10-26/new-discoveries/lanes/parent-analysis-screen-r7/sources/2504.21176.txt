                                             NUMBERS OF THE FIRST KIND: TREES, LATTICES, AND GAMES

arXiv:2504.21176v3 [math.CO] 25 Sep 2025
                                                   Abstract. We study an alternating sum involving factorials and Stirling numbers of the
                                                   first kind. We give an exponential generating function for these numbers and show they are
                                                   nonnegative and enumerate the number of increasing trees on n vertices that are won by the
                                                   second of two players when interpreted as a game tree. We also give a simple description
                                                   of the quotient from the weak order to the Tamari lattice in terms of plane trees, and give
                                                   bijections between plane trees, 213-avoiding permutations, and 312-avoiding permutations.
                                                   Finally, for a rooted tree, we give equivalent characterizations of when it describes a game
                                                   won by the first or second player in terms of the rank-generating function of the lattice of
                                                   prunings and the Euler characteristic of an associated real variety.

   For any permutation of n with k cycles, the number (k − 1)! counts the number of re-
orderings of its cycle decomposition such that the cycle containing 1 is first. When writing
the cycle decomposition of a permutation, we will write the smallest number in each cycle
first. As an example, the (3 − 1)! = 2 reorderings of the cycle decomposition (12)(364)(5)
counted are itself and (12)(5)(364).

125364 in one-line notation. Therefore, if we let Wn denote the signed set of permutations in

              3. Plane trees and a quotient to the Tamari lattice
   In this section, we make an excursion to show that ρ ◦ γ induces a lattice quotient from
the weak order on S1 × Sn−1 to the Tamari lattice described via plane trees. The weak
order is a lattice structure on S1 × Sn−1 where α ≤ β if and only if the inversion set of α
is contained in the inversion set of β. Write Inv(α) for the set of inversions of α. A lattice
quotient is a surjective map of lattices that is order-preserving and preserves joins and meets.
The Tamari lattice, first introduced by Tamari in his 1951 thesis, was shown to arise from a
lattice quotient of the weak order in [8].
   We begin by describing stack-based algorithms to label plane trees. We will write plane(n)
to denote the set of plane trees on n vertices. A stack is a “last in, first out” data structure,
analogous to a stack of books on a table. It has two operations: push, which adds an element
to the stack, and pop, which removes the element in the stack added last. In this section,
when we include an additional parameter i to the push or pop operations of a stack, we will
understand it to mean to label the vertex being pushed or popped with the label i.
   Our first algorithm takes in the root of a plane tree and gives the eastpush-labelling of the
plane tree.

   For this section, our analysis will be simplified if we consider the root of our plane tree
to have a parent. That way, the notion of grandparent is well-defined for all vertices other
than the root. We also extend our notion of left, so we say that v is left of w if and only if
v is contained in a subtree rooted at a vertex that is a left sibling of the root of a subtree
containing w. Note v appears before w in the postorder traversal if and only if either v is
left of w or w is an ancestor of v.
Lemma 3.2. For T ∈ plane(n), the vertices labelled before a given vertex in the eastpush-
labelling of T are
   (1) all vertices right of its parent,
   (2) all children of non-parent ancestors, and
   (3) all left siblings.
Proof. We proceed by induction. The statement is vacuously true for the root of T .
   For the inductive step, consider a vertex being pushed onto the stack. By the time its
parent was pushed onto the stack, the vertices that had been labelled were all vertices right
of the vertex’s grandparent, all children of ancestors that are not the vertex’s parent or
grandparent, all left siblings of the parent, and the parent.
   Because the vertex’s grandparent’s children were pushed from left to right, the parent
is popped after all of its right siblings are popped. Between the parent being pushed and
popped, the algorithm traverses all subtrees rooted at right siblings of the parent, and labels
everything right of the vertex’s parent but not right of the vertex’s grandparent.
   Finally, when the parent is being processed, before the vertex is pushed, all left siblings
of the vertex are pushed onto the stack. Putting this altogether, we see that the vertices
labelled before the vertex is pushed onto the stack consist of everything right of its parent,
all children of nonparent ancestors, and all left siblings.                                  □

Lemma 3.4. For T ∈ plane(n), the vertices labelled before a given vertex in the westpop-
labelling of T are
   (1) all vertices to its left, and
   (2) all ancestors.
Proof. We proceed by induction. The statement is vacuously true for the root of T .
   For the inductive step, consider a vertex being popped off of the stack. By the time its
parent was being processed, the vertices that had been labelled were all vertices left of the
vertex’s parent and all ancestors. While the parent was being processed, the vertex and its
siblings are pushed onto the stack from right to left.

at them must be processed by the algorithm. So before the vertex is popped, the vertices
that have already been labelled are all vertices to its left and all ancestors.             □
  By Lemmas 3.2 and 3.4, the eastpush- and westpop-labellings of a plane tree have labels
increasing among children of a vertex from left to right and from parents to children. Let ε, ω :
plane(n) → inc(n) denote the eastpush- and westpop-labellings of a plane tree, respectively.
These labellings are related by γ −1 to 213- and 312-avoiding permutations. A permutation
α ∈ S1 × Sn−1 avoids 213 (respectively 312) if there exists indices i < j < k such that
α(i), α(j), α(k) are in the same relative order as 2, 1, 3 (respectively, 3, 1, 2).
Proposition 3.5. For T ∈ plane(n),
  (1) γ −1 (ε(T )) is 213-avoiding, and
  (2) γ −1 (ω(T )) is 312-avoiding.
Proof. For part (1), suppose for a contradiction that i < j < k and the postorder traversal
of T visits the vertices labelled in ε(T ) by j, i, k in that order. Since k > i, j and k is visited
last among i, j, k in the postorder traversal while ε(T ) is an increasing tree, k must be right
of i and j. Since i, j are labelled before k in the eastpush-labelling of T , i and j must then
be children of ancestors of k by Lemma 3.2, but are not ancestors themselves. Thus neither
i nor j is a descendant of the other, and so j is left of i.
   However, i cannot be right of j’s parent, because i is left of k and j’s parent is an ancestor
of k. i cannot be a child of a non-parent ancestor of j, because then since i is left of k, the
child of the ancestor that is the root of the subtree containing j must be right of i, and so j
would be right of i. And i is not a left sibling of j. This contradicts Lemma 3.2.
   For part (2), suppose for a contradiction that i < j < k and the postorder traversal of T
visits the vertices labelled in ω(T ) by k, i, j, in that order. By Lemma 3.4, this requires that
i must be left of j, and i, j are ancestors of k. This is already a contradiction, since i must
then either be an ancestor or a descendant of j.                                                  □

For α ∈ S1 × Sn−1 , the first inversion function of α is the function tα : {2, . . . , n + 1} →

inversion from position i and t(i) = n + 1 if there are no inversions from position i.
Lemma 3.8. For 213-avoiding α ∈ S1 × Sn−1 , Inv(α) = {(i, j) | tα (i) ≤ j ≤ n}.

there are no other inversions in α.                                                            □

  We call the equivalence relation induced on the fibers of a lattice quotient a lattice con-
gruence.

Proposition 3.11. [8, Section 3] An equivalence relation on a lattice L is a lattice congruence
if and only if all of the following hold:
     (1) each equivalence class is an interval,
     (2) the map π ↑ from L sending an element to the top of its equivalence class is order-
         preserving, and
     (3) the map π↓ from L sending an element to the bottom of its equivalence class is
         order-preserving.

Theorem 3.12. The map ρ ◦ γ : S1 × Sn−1 → plane(n) induces the Tamari lattice structure
on plane(n) respecting the lattice structure of the weak order on S1 × Sn−1 , making ρ ◦ γ a
lattice quotient.

Proof. We will show that the fibers of ρ ◦ γ form a lattice congruence. That the lattice
structure on plane trees is the Tamari lattice then follows from [2, Section 9], where they
identify the lattice on 213-avoiding permutations in the weak order with the Tamari lattice.
   Condition (1) of Proposition 3.11 is Proposition 3.10. For condition (2), let α ≤ β ∈
S1 × Sn−1 and let π ↑ (α), π ↑ (β) denote the greatest elements in the fibers of ρ ◦ γ containing
α, β, respectively. Since α ≤ β in the weak order, we see that each tβ (i) ≤ tα (i). Therefore,
{(i, j) | tα (i) ≤ j ≤ n} ⊆ {(i, j) | tβ (i) ≤ j ≤ n}, and so π ↑ (α) ≤ π ↑ (β) in the weak
order. The proof of condition (3) is similar. (To see that tα (i) lies in {tkβ (i)}k≥1 , note for all
i ≤ j < tα (i) that tβ (j) ≤ tα (j) ≤ tα (i).)                                                     □

Remark 3.13. In terms of first inversion functions, the join and meet operators of the Tamari
lattice can deduced from (tα∨β )(i) = min(tα (i), tβ (i)) and (tα∧β )(i) = min({tkα (i)}k≥1 ∩
{tkβ (i)}k≥1 ). First inversion functions are shifts of the bracketing functions studied by Huang
and Tamari in [4], but the meet is not so easily described using bracketing functions, and so
was omitted from their paper.
   The description of the Tamari lattice in terms of plane trees can be shown to be equivalent
to Knuth’s description in terms of ordered forests (plane trees with the root removed) in [6,
Section 7.2.1.6]. The description of the quotient from the weak order to the Tamari lattice
described using plane trees via first inversions appears to be new.

player with the winning strategy is equivalent to evaluating φT (−1).
