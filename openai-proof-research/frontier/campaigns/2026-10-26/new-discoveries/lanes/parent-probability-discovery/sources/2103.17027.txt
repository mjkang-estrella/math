                                           1     Introduction
                                           Suppose we sample an urn of n balls, each coloured red with probability p and otherwise
                                           blue. What is the probability that a sample of k balls, with replacement, from this urn
                                           consists of only red balls? Such questions are of interest to sample-eﬃcient statistics and
                                           the derandomisation of algorithms.
                                               If R ∼ Binomial(n, p) denotes the number of red balls in the urn, the probability of
                                           drawing a single red ball from the urn is R/n. Thus, the probability that a sample of k
                                           balls from the urn is all red is given by (R/n)k , or P = E[(R/n)k ] when the probability is
                                           taken over both sample phases. Whenever the urn is large (n is large), R/n concentrates
                                           around p, so sampling from the urn is equivalent to sampling from the original distribution
                                           and P ≈ pk . Indeed, from Jensen’s inequality, we can see that pk is always a lower bound:
                                           P = E[(R/n)k ] ≥ E[(R/n)]k = pk . Previous authors have shown a nearly matching upper
                                           bound of C k pk in the range k/(np) = O(1) for some constant C > 1. (See eq. (1) below
                                           for details.) In this note, we improve the upper bound to P ≤ pk (1 + k/(2np))k , which
                                                                     √
                                           shows that when k = o( np), the factor C k can be replaced by just 1 + o(1).

2    Bounds
The theorem considers “sub-Poissonian” random variables, which are variables X, satis-
fying the requirement E[exp(tX)] ≤ exp(µ(et − 1)). Such sub-Poissonian include many
simple distributions, such as the Poisson or Binomial distribution. We give more examples
in Section 3.
