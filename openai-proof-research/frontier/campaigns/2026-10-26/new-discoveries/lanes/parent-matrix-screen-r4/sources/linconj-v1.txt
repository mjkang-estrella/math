        Abstract. Let A and B be positive semidefinite, and let 0 ≤ t ≤ 1. Lin (2017) conjectured that
                                                      1/2
                               λj ((1 − t)A + tB) ≥ σj (A2(1−t) B 2t ),     j = 1, . . . , n.
        The case t = 12 is Drury’s theorem, which answered a long-standing question of Bhatia and Kittaneh.
        Lin remarked that Drury’s method does not seem to reach other values of t. We prove his conjecture
        for every t ∈ [0, 1]. The proof builds on Drury’s argument, but replaces his block-matrix step (unique
        to the midpoint t = 12 ) by an eigenvalue count based on an inertia identity and a one-variable
        inequality.



                                               1. I NTRODUCTION
  Finding matrix analogues of scalar inequalities has long been a theme of matrix analysis. Many
works study analogues of the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality; see, e.g., the books (Bhatia,
1997, 2007; Zhan, 2002). Some of the most useful of these inequalities compare singular values σ(·)
and eigenvalues λ(·) of matrices. A fundamental example of such an inequality is
                         1/2
(1.1)                   σj (AB) ≤ 12 λj (A + B),                A, B ⪰ 0,       j = 1, . . . , n,
which was raised as a question by Bhatia and Kittaneh (2000) and proved twelve years later by Drury
(2012).
   Bhatia and Kittaneh (1990) proved the weaker inequality σj (A1/2 B 1/2 ) ≤ 12 λj (A + B). This
inequality was generalized by Ando (1995) to σj (A1−t B t ) ≤ λj ((1−t)A+tB) for t ∈ [0, 1]. Revisiting
Drury’s proof, Lin (2017, Conjecture 5.2) conjectured the corresponding weighted form of (1.1),
and wrote that Drury’s method “does not seem to lead to a solution.” To our knowledge no proof
has appeared since, and Theorem 1.1 supplies one.
Theorem 1.1 (Lin’s conjecture). Let A, B ⪰ 0 and let 0 ≤ t ≤ 1. Then, for every j = 1, . . . , n,
                                      1/2
(1.2)                               σj (A2(1−t) B 2t ) ≤ λj ((1 − t)A + tB).
  More broadly, both Ando’s inequality and Theorem 1.1 are members of the family
(1.3)                    σjq (A(1−t)/q B t/q ) ≤ λj ((1 − t)A + tB),           j = 1, . . . , n,
indexed by q > 0. A brief calculation shows that if inequality (1.3) holds for an exponent q, then it
also holds for every larger exponent, so the statements become stronger as q decreases. The cases
q ≥ 1 therefore follow from Ando’s inequality, while Lin’s conjecture is the case q = 1/2, outside
that range. Theorem 1.1 gives (1.3) for every q ≥ 1/2, and another brief calculation shows that at
t = 1/2 the exponent 1/2 is the best.

2.1. Rescaling. Using a and b, inequality (1.2) reads

If t = 0 or t = 1 the inequality is trivial; so we assume t ∈ (0, 1). Also, it suffices to consider
A, B ≻ 0 (the ⪰ 0 cases follow by continuity). We can also assume a ≤ b (otherwise flip the roles
of A and B and note σ(AB) = σ(BA)), so that b ≥ 1/2. Fix j ∈ {1, . . . , n}. If σj (A2a B 2b ) = 0,
then (2.1) is trivial; otherwise by homogeneity we may scale (2.1) so that σj (A2a B 2b ) = 1, and
reduce the task to proving that λj (aA + bB) ≥ 1.

2.3. Eigenvalue count. Inequality (2.5) says that aA + bB0 − I has at least j nonnegative
eigenvalues. This matrix is H + bB0 with H = aA − I: a Hermitian matrix, in general indefinite,
plus a positive semidefinite perturbation carried by the j-dimensional space Ran P . The following
inertia identity transfers such an eigenvalue count to a matrix that acts only on the space carrying
the perturbation. It is the finite-dimensional form of the Birman–Schwinger principle (Schwinger,
1961).

Let J : P → H be an isometric inclusion, and let W ≻ 0 on P. Set

Then

where n≥0 counts eigenvalues ≥ 0, n+ counts eigenvalues > 0, and n≤0 counts eigenvalues ≤ 0, each
with multiplicity.
