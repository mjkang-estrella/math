                                                           OPTIMAL CONSTANTS IN NORMED PLANES

                                                           VITOR BALESTRO, HORST MARTINI, AND RALPH TEIXEIRA

                                                  Abstract. We define new geometric constants for normed planes, determine their opti-
                                                  mal values, and characterize types of planes for which these optimal values are attained.
                                                  Relations of these constants to several topics, such as areas and distances from points
                                                  to sides of triangles, are also investigated. We perform some calculations of new and old
                                                  constants given by trigonometric functions for certain classes of norms, and a conjecture
                                                  on a certain geometric constant is disproved.

    Throughout the text, (V, || · ||) will always denote a Minkowski or normed plane. Its
unit ball and unit circle are denoted by B := {x : kxk ≤ 1} and S := {x : kxk = 1},
respectively. A nondegenerate symplectic bilinear form in V will be denoted by [·, ·], and
|| · ||a stands for the associated antinorm, i.e., for x ∈ V we have kxka = sup{|[y, x]| : y ∈
S} (see [15]). As usual, we say that a vector x ∈ V is Birkhoff orthogonal to a vector
y ∈ V (and denote this by x ⊣B y) when ||x + ty|| ≥ ||x|| for all t ∈ R, and we call two
vectors x, y ∈ V isosceles orthogonal whenever ||x + y|| = ||x − y||. The sine function

higher dimensional analogue of a normed plane, namely a finite dimensional real Banach
space, is called a Minkowski space. For basic concepts and further background regarding
the geometry of Minkowski planes and spaces we refer the reader to the book [23] and to

                                    2. The constant cD
   Two directions x, y ∈ V are said to be conjugate if they are mutually Birkhoff orthog-
onal, i.e., if x ⊣B y and y ⊣B x. It is well known that if any Birkhoff orthogonal pair of
directions is conjugate (i.e., if Birkhoff orthogonality is a symmetric relation), then the
plane is Radon and vice versa (see [15] for many references and some background). It is
proved in [16] that every normed plane contains at least one pair of conjugate directions.
On the other hand, in any non-Radon plane we must have a pair of directions x, y ∈ V
such that x ⊣B y, but the converse does not hold. Hence, a way to estimate how far a
plane is from being Radon is to measure somehow the worst case of those planes where x
is Birkhoff orthogonal to y, but y is not Birkhoff orthogonal to x. We define the constant
                       cD (|| · ||) := inf{s(y, x) : x, y ∈ S and x ⊣B y}.
Notice that cD is in fact attained for a pair x, y ∈ S. This comes immediately from the
compactness of the set {(y, x) ∈ S × S : x ⊣B y} and from the continuity of the sine
function s.

  Another trigonometric constant, for quantifying how far a plane is from being Radon,
was defined in [4]. The fact that the sine function is symmetric only for Radon planes
motivated the definition of the constant

                                           x,y∈S

both constants.

                                 3. The constants ct and cT
  Let (V, || · ||) be a Radon plane, and let ∆ be an equilateral triangle such that its sides
are the vectors x, y, and z. In [4] it is proven that s(x, y) = s(y, z) = s(z, x) and hence we
may denote this number by s(∆). We define the constants ct and cT to be the infimum
and the supremum of the set R||·|| := {s(∆) : ∆ ⊆ (V, || · ||) is equilateral}, respectively.

   Notice that, since the sine function is homogeneous, we have that the infimum and the
supremum may be taken over the equilateral triangles with unit sides. In order to study
these constants, we begin with equilateral triangles in Radon planes. Our main objective
is to prove that in any Radon plane the constants ct and cT are indeed attained for some
triangles.
Proposition 3.1 ([16], Proposition 33). Given any segment [pq] in a Minkowski plane,
and a half-plane determined by the line hpqi. Then there exists a point r in the half-plane
such that ∆pqr is an equilateral triangle. The point r is unique if and only if the longest
segment in the unit circle parallel to hpqi has length at most 1.
Lemma 3.1. Let [pq] ⊆ S be a segment of the unit circle S of a Radon plane (V, || · ||).
Then ||p − q|| ≤ 1, and equality implies that S is an affine regular hexagon.

Remark. Notice that in the last proposition we did not require that (V, || · ||) is Radon,

Theorem 3.1. Let (V, || · ||) be a normed plane. The following statements are equivalent:

(a) The plane (V, k · k) is Radon and cT (|| · ||) = 1.

(b) There exist three distinct directions x, y, z ∈ V such that x ⊣B y, y ⊣B z, and z ⊣B x.

(c) The unit circle is an affine regular hexagon.

(d) There exists an equilateral triangle with unit sides for which the sum of distances from
any interior point to its sides equals 1.

Proof. The bi-implication (b) ⇔ (c) is Proposition 3.3. If (a) holds, then there exists an
equilateral triangle with sides lying in directions x, y, and z such that s(x, y) = s(y, z) =
s(z, x) = 1, and hence we have (b) and then (c). The implications (c) ⇒ (a) and (a) ⇒
(d) come easily, and thus it just remains to prove that (d) ⇒ (a). For this, recall that
the distance of a point p to a line l in the direction q is attained for a segment [pr], r ∈ l,
such that (p − r) ⊣B q. The equilateral triangle described in (d) is such that the distance
from each vertex to the supporting line of the respective opposite side is attained by the
other two sides. Hence, if its sides lie in the directions x, y, and z, it follows in particular
that x ⊣B y, y ⊣B z, and z ⊣B x. This finishes the proof.

    Interlude 1: a curious difference between regular (4n)-gonal and
                           (4n + 2)-gonal norms
  While regular (4n+2)-gons are always Radon curves (see [8] and [15]), the regular (4n)-
gons are equiframed curves (see [14]), which are centrally symmetric closed convex curves
with the property that each of their points is touched by a circumscribed parallelogram
of smallest area. In [14], Martini and Swanepoel argued that, in some sense, Radon
curves and equiframed curves behave dually. Namely, Radon curves can be defined as the
centrally symmetric closed convex curves each point of which is a vertex of an inscribed
parallelogram of largest area. We establish now another kind of “dual behavior” of regular
(4n)-gons and (4n + 2)-gons: in some sense, regular (4n)-gons preserve Euclidean central
angles, while regular (4n + 2)-gons preserve Euclidean side lengths.

                 Interlude 2: Discussing a conjecture from [9]
   In [9], Remark 13, the authors conjectured the existence of a normed space (V, || · ||)
for which there are no x, y ∈ S with x ⊣I y such that D(V ) = inf t∈R ||x + ty||. We use
the sine function approach to prove that such a finite dimensional space does not exist.
Proposition 4.2. Let (V, || · ||) be a Minkowski space. Then there exist x, y ∈ S with
x ⊣I y such that D(V ) = s(x, y).
Proof. The set I ⊆ S × S given by
                               I = {(x, y) ∈ S × S : x ⊣I y}
is compact. In [4] it is proved that the sine function s : S × S → R is continuous in
planes, but the proof can obviously be extended to higher dimensional spaces. It follows
that D(V ) is the infimum of the compact image s(I), and the proof is finished.
