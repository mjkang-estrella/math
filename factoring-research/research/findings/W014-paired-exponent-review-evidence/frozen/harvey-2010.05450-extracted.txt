An exponent one-fifth algorithm for deterministic integer factorisation




David Harvey

Email address: d.harvey@unsw.edu.au

Address: School of Mathematics and Statistics, University of New South Wales, Sydney NSW 2052, Australia



Abstract.
    

Hittmeir recently presented a deterministic algorithm that provably computes
the prime factorisation of a positive integer NN
in N2/9+o⁡(1)N^{2/9+o(1)} bit operations.
Prior to this breakthrough, the best known complexity bound
for this problem was N1/4+o⁡(1)N^{1/4+o(1)},
a result going back to the 1970s.
In this paper we push Hittmeir’s techniques further,
obtaining a rigorous, deterministic factoring algorithm
with complexity N1/5+o⁡(1)N^{1/5+o(1)}.

  




1. Introduction





Let 𝖥⁡(N)\FF(N) denote the time required to compute the prime factorisation
of an integer N⩾2N\geqslant 2.
By “time” we mean “number of bit operations”,
or more precisely, the number of steps performed by a deterministic
Turing machine with a fixed, finite number of linear tapes
[Pap94].
All integers are assumed to be encoded in the usual binary representation.





In this paper we prove the following result:




Theorem 1.1.



There is an integer factorisation algorithm achieving






𝖥⁡(N)=O⁡(N1/5​log16/5​N).\FF(N)=O(N^{1/5}\log^{16/5}N).








One may show that the space complexity of this algorithm is
O⁡(N1/5​log11/5​N)O(N^{1/5}\log^{11/5}N),
but we will not give the details of this analysis.





We emphasise that the new algorithm is deterministic,
the complexity bound is rigorously established,
and the algorithm runs on a classical computer.
If one is willing to relax these conditions,
better complexity bounds are known:



– 



The class group relations method of Lenstra and Pomerance [LP92]
provably runs in expected time






exp⁡((1+o⁡(1))​(log⁡N)1/2​(log⁡log⁡N)1/2).\exp\big((1+o(1))(\log N)^{1/2}(\log\log N)^{1/2}\big).




This is the best known rigorous bound for a probabilistic factoring algorithm.




– 



The general number field sieve is conjectured to run in time






exp⁡(((64/9)1/3+o⁡(1))​(log⁡N)1/3​(log⁡log⁡N)2/3).\exp\big(((64/9)^{1/3}+o(1))(\log N)^{1/3}(\log\log N)^{2/3}\big).




See [CP05, §6.2] for a discussion of the heuristics
supporting this conjecture.
This algorithm is responsible for all recent record-breaking factorisations,
such as the factorisation of the 250-digit challenge number RSA-250
[BGG+20].




– 



Shor’s factoring algorithm runs in polynomial time,
specifically (log⁡N)2+o⁡(1)(\log N)^{2+o(1)}, on a quantum computer
[Sho94].








It is also worth mentioning the deterministic algorithm of
Shanks [Sha71],
which achieves 𝖥⁡(N)=N1/5+o⁡(1)\FF(N)=N^{1/5+o(1)},
conditional on the (still unproved) Generalised Riemann Hypothesis.
According to [CP05, §5.6.4],
the complexity of this algorithm is actually N1/5​log4+o⁡(1)​NN^{1/5}\log^{4+o(1)}N,
which is slightly worse than our bound in Theorem 1.1.





The literature on integer factorisation is vast.
For further pointers, see [CP05].
For the rest of the paper, we focus on algorithms that are
deterministic, rigorous, and classical.





The naive trial division method,
i.e., checking all possible divisors up to N1/2N^{1/2},
runs in time N1/2+o⁡(1)N^{1/2+o(1)}.
The first algorithm faster than this was that of Lehman [Leh74],
who obtained 𝖥⁡(N)=N1/3+o⁡(1)\FF(N)=N^{1/3+o(1)}.
Lehman’s main theorem shows that if NN is a semiprime,
i.e., N=p​qN=pq for distinct primes pp and qq,
then there exists a pair of “small” integers aa and bb such that
a​q+b​paq+bp lies in a certain short interval (depending on aa and bb).
His algorithm examines every integer in this interval,
for every aa and bb in a suitable range,
and succeeds in factoring NN as soon as it encounters a​q+b​paq+bp.





Shortly after Lehman’s work appeared,
Pollard [Pol74] and Strassen [Str77]
independently published two closely related algorithms achieving
𝖥⁡(N)=N1/4+o⁡(1)\FF(N)=N^{1/4+o(1)}.
Both of these algorithms depend heavily on the FFT
(fast Fourier transform).
Strassen’s method was revisited by Bostan, Gaudry and Schost
[BGS07],
and then again by Costa and the present author [CH14],
who achieved






𝖥⁡(N)=O⁡(N1/4​log2​N(log⁡log⁡N)1/2).\FF(N)=O\left(\frac{N^{1/4}\log^{2}N}{(\log\log N)^{1/2}}\right).

(1.1)



(The results in [BGS07] and [CH14]
were actually stated in terms of the function 𝖬⁡(n)\MM(n),
the cost of multiplying nn-bit integers.
It is now known that 𝖬⁡(n)=O⁡(n​log⁡n)\MM(n)=O(n\log n) [HvdH19],
and at the risk of anachronism,
we will use this result in our historical discussion to simplify
several complexity statements from the literature.)





Another family of algorithms achieving 𝖥⁡(N)=N1/4+o⁡(1)\FF(N)=N^{1/4+o(1)}
is based on Coppersmith’s method [Cop97]
for finding small roots of polynomial equations modulo NN
(see also [Ber08, §5]).
These algorithms rely on fast lattice reduction techniques such as LLL.
Their complexity is presumably of the form O⁡(N1/4​logk​N)O(N^{1/4}\log^{k}N)
for some small positive integer kk,
but the author is not aware of a precise statement in the literature.
This approach has the advantage that the space complexity is only
(log⁡N)O⁡(1)(\log N)^{O(1)},
comparing favourably to the N1/4+o⁡(1)N^{1/4+o(1)} space complexity of the
Pollard–Strassen method.





In 2016, Hittmeir established the bound






𝖥⁡(N)=O⁡(N1/4​exp⁡(−C1​log⁡Nlog⁡(C2​log⁡N))​log2​N),\FF(N)=O\left(N^{1/4}\exp\left(\frac{-C_{1}\log N}{\log(C_{2}\log N)}\right)\log^{2}N\right),

(1.2)



for certain explicit constants C1,C2>0C_{1},C_{2}>0 [Hit18].
This bound is still N1/4+o⁡(1)N^{1/4+o(1)},
but improves on the “optimised Strassen bound” (1.1) by a superpolynomial factor,
i.e., by an arbitrarily large power of log⁡N\log N.
Hittmeir’s algorithm relies on an trick that seems to have first been suggested
by Pollard [Pol78, §3]:
if NN is a semiprime p​qpq, then Euler’s theorem implies that






αp+q≡αN+1(modN)\alpha^{p+q}\equiv\alpha^{N+1}\pmod{N}

(1.3)



for any α∈ℤN∗\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{*}.
One may therefore try to compute p+qp+q (and hence factor NN)
by solving αx≡αN+1(modN)\alpha^{x}\equiv\alpha^{N+1}\pmod{N},
using for instance Shanks’ BSGS (baby-step/giant-step) algorithm
for computing discrete logarithms [Sha71].
McKee and Pinch [MP96, §2] turn this idea into a deterministic
factoring algorithm with 𝖥⁡(N)=N1/3+o⁡(1)\FF(N)=N^{1/3+o(1)},
but Hittmeir squeezes much more out of it.
Among other things, he shows that the relation p​q=Npq=N forces certain
restrictions on the residue of p+qp+q modulo various small primes,
which can be used to speed up the BSGS search.





More recently, in a spectacular preprint [Hit20],
Hittmeir presented an algorithm achieving






𝖥⁡(N)=O⁡(N2/9​log11/3​N).\FF(N)=O(N^{2/9}\log^{11/3}N).




Notice that 2/9=0.222​…<0.25=1/42/9=0.222\ldots<0.25=1/4.
This result represents the first exponential speedup for the problem of
integer factorisation since the 1970s.
The algorithm has many features in common with [Hit18],
but instead of searching for p+qp+q,
it searches for the linear combinations a​q+b​paq+bp
that arise in Lehman’s algorithm.
The replacement for (1.3) in this context
is the congruence






αa​q+b​p≡αa​N+b(modp),\alpha^{aq+bp}\equiv\alpha^{aN+b}\pmod{p},




which follows from Fermat’s little theorem.
His algorithm actually examines many more candidates than Lehman’s algorithm,
but the complexity is drastically reduced thanks to the use of the BSGS search.





The algorithm described in the present paper achieves a further exponential
speedup, from N2/9+o⁡(1)N^{2/9+o(1)} to N1/5+o⁡(1)=N0.2+o⁡(1)N^{1/5+o(1)}=N^{0.2+o(1)}.
It is based largely on Hittmeir’s algorithm from [Hit20].
The main difference, and the source of the exponential speedup,
is that we apply the BSGS search technique much more aggressively.
Whereas Hittmeir breaks up the search space into chunks and
applies the BSGS search to each chunk separately,
we show how to sweep through the entire space with a single BSGS search.
For a more quantitative discussion of the
relationship between the two algorithms,
see Remark 3.4.





We also make one other small improvement to Hittmeir’s algorithm:
we replace a certain multipoint evaluation step with an invocation of
Bluestein’s algorithm [Blu70] (see Algorithm 4.1).
This saves a further factor of (log⁡N)O⁡(1)(\log N)^{O(1)} overall.






2. Preliminaries





In this section we introduce various notation and terminology,
and recall several ingredients from the literature that will be needed later.
All results stated in this section without specific references may be found
in standard textbooks on computer algebra, such as
[vzGG13] or [BZ11].





For nn a positive integer, we define
lg⁡n≔⌈log⁡n/log⁡2⌉\lg n\coloneqq\lceil\log n/\log 2\rceil if n⩾2n\geqslant 2,
and lg⁡1≔1\lg 1\coloneqq 1.
Clearly lg⁡n=Θ⁡(log⁡n)\lg n=\Theta(\log n) as n→∞n\to\infty.





Given a list of n⩾1n\geqslant 1 elements of bit size β⩾1\beta\geqslant 1,
we may sort the list using the “merge sort” algorithm
in time O⁡(n​β​lg⁡n)O(n\beta\lg n) [Knu98].
Here we assume that the elements belong to some totally ordered set,
and that we may compare a pair of elements in time O⁡(β)O(\beta).





2.1. Integer arithmetic





Let n⩾1n\geqslant 1, and assume that we are given integers xx and yy such that
|x|,|y|⩽2n|x|,|y|\leqslant 2^{n}.



• 



We may compute x+yx+y and x−yx-y in time O⁡(n)O(n).




• 



We write 𝖬⁡(n)\MM(n) for the cost of computing the product x​yxy.
As mentioned in the Introduction, we may take
𝖬⁡(n)=O⁡(n​lg⁡n)\MM(n)=O(n\lg n) [HvdH19].




• 



If y>0y>0, we may compute the quotients ⌊x/y⌋\lfloor x/y\rfloor and
⌈x/y⌉\lceil x/y\rceil in time O⁡(𝖬⁡(n))O(\MM(n)).
We may therefore also compute the residue of xx modulo yy
in the interval [0,y)[0,y) in time O⁡(𝖬⁡(n))O(\MM(n)).




• 



More generally, for a fixed positive rational number u/vu/v,
and assuming that x,y>0x,y>0, we may compute
⌊(x/y)u/v⌋\lfloor(x/y)^{u/v}\rfloor and ⌈(x/y)u/v⌉\lceil(x/y)^{u/v}\rceil
in time O⁡(𝖬⁡(n))O(\MM(n)).




• 



Using the “half-GCD” algorithm,
we may compute g≔gcd⁡(x,y)g\coloneqq\gcd(x,y),
and if desired find integers uu and vv such that u​x+v​y=gux+vy=g,
in time O⁡(𝖬⁡(n)​lg⁡n)=O⁡(n​lg2​n)O(\MM(n)\lg n)=O(n\lg^{2}n).
In particular, if gcd⁡(x,y)=1\gcd(x,y)=1,
we may compute the inverse of xx modulo yy
in time O⁡(𝖬⁡(n)​lg⁡n)O(\MM(n)\lg n).








Let N⩾2N\geqslant 2.
We write ℤN\mathbb{Z}_{N} for the ring ℤ/N​ℤ\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z} of integers modulo NN.
Elements of ℤN\mathbb{Z}_{N} will always be represented by their residues in the
interval [0,N)[0,N).
In particular, an element of ℤN\mathbb{Z}_{N} occupies at most lg⁡N\lg N bits of storage.
We write ℤN∗\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{*} for the group of units of ℤN\mathbb{Z}_{N},
i.e., the subset of those x∈ℤNx\in\mathbb{Z}_{N} for which gcd⁡(x,N)=1\gcd(x,N)=1.






• 



Given x,y∈ℤNx,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{N},
we may compute x±y∈ℤNx\pm y\in\mathbb{Z}_{N} in time O⁡(lg⁡N)O(\lg N),
and we may compute x​y∈ℤNxy\in\mathbb{Z}_{N} in time O⁡(𝖬⁡(lg⁡N))=O⁡(lg⁡N​lg⁡lg⁡N)O(\MM(\lg N))=O(\lg N\lg\lg N).




• 



Given x∈ℤNx\in\mathbb{Z}_{N} and an integer m⩾0m\geqslant 0,
we may compute xm∈ℤNx^{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{N} in time O⁡(𝖬⁡(lg⁡N)​lg⁡m)O(\MM(\lg N)\lg m),
using the “repeated squaring” algorithm.




• 



Given x∈ℤNx\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}, we may test whether x∈ℤN∗x\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{*}
in time O⁡(𝖬⁡(lg⁡N)​lg⁡lg​N)O(\MM(\lg N)\lg\lg N), by computing gcd⁡(x,N)\gcd(x,N).
If this GCD is not 11 or NN,
and if N=p​qN=pq is a semiprime,
then we immediately recover pp and qq in time O⁡(𝖬⁡(lg⁡N))O(\MM(\lg N)).









2.2. Polynomial arithmetic





We always represent a polynomial in ℤN​[x]\mathbb{Z}_{N}[x] by a list of its coefficients.
For d⩾1d\geqslant 1, let 𝖬N⁡(d)\MM_{N}(d) denote the cost of multiplying two polynomials
in ℤN​[x]\mathbb{Z}_{N}[x] of degree at most dd.




Lemma 2.1.



If d=O⁡(N)d=O(N) then






𝖬N⁡(d)=O⁡(d​lg2​N).\MM_{N}(d)=O(d\lg^{2}N).

(2.1)






Proof.



Using Kronecker substitution, i.e., packing the coefficients into large
integers and then multiplying these integers
(see [vzGG13, Cor. 8.27]), we obtain






𝖬N⁡(d)=O⁡(𝖬⁡(d⁡(lg⁡N+lg⁡d))).\MM_{N}(d)=O(\MM(d(\lg N+\lg d))).




Under the hypothesis d=O⁡(N)d=O(N) this simplifies to O⁡(d​lg2​N)O(d\lg^{2}N).
∎





Remark 2.2.



In the above lemma,
the hypothesis “d=O⁡(N)d=O(N)” means that for every constant C>0C>0,
the lemma holds for all dd and NN in the region d<C​Nd<CN,
where the implied big-OO constant in (2.1)
may depend on CC.
For the rest of the paper, a similar remark applies whenever we use
the big-OO notation in this way.





Lemma 2.3.



Let n⩾1n\geqslant 1 with n=O⁡(N)n=O(N).
Given as input v1,…,vn∈ℤNv_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{N},
we may compute the polynomial
f(x)=(x−v1)⋯(x−vn)∈ℤN[x]f(x)=(x-v_{1})\cdots(x-v_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}[x]
in time






O⁡(n​lg3​N).O(n\lg^{3}N).







Proof.



This is a standard application of a product tree:
starting with the linear polynomials x−vix-v_{i},
we repeatedly multiply pairs of polynomials together,
with the degree doubling at each level.
There are O⁡(lg⁡n)=O⁡(lg⁡N)O(\lg n)=O(\lg N) levels,
and Lemma 2.1 implies that
the cost at each level is O⁡(n​lg2​N)O(n\lg^{2}N).
For further details, see for instance [vzGG13, Algorithm 10.3].
∎





Lemma 2.4.



Given as input an element α∈ℤN∗\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{N},
positive integers m,n=O⁡(N)m,n=O(N),
and f∈ℤN​[x]f\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}[x] of degree nn,
we may compute f⁡(1),f⁡(α),…,f⁡(αm−1)∈ℤNf(1),f(\alpha),\ldots,f(\alpha^{m-1})\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
in time






O⁡((n+m)​lg2​N).O((n+m)\lg^{2}N).







Proof.



We use a variant of Bluestein’s trick [Blu70]
(following [HvdH18, Lem. 3]).
Let f⁡(x)=∑j=0nfj​xjf(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}f_{j}x^{j}.
Then the identity i​j=(i2)+(−j2)−(i−j2)ij=\binom{i}{2}+\binom{-j}{2}-\binom{i-j}{2}
implies that






f⁡(αi)=∑j=0nfj​αi​j=hi​∑j=0nfj′​gi−j,f(\alpha^{i})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}f_{j}\alpha^{ij}=h_{i}\sum_{j=0}^{n}f^{\prime}_{j}g_{i-j},

(2.2)



where






hi≔α(i2),fj′≔α(−j2)​fj,gk≔α−(k2).h_{i}\coloneqq\alpha^{\binom{i}{2}},\qquad f^{\prime}_{j}\coloneqq\alpha^{\binom{-j}{2}}f_{j},\qquad g_{k}\coloneqq\alpha^{-\binom{k}{2}}.




Using the identities (i+12)=(i2)+i\binom{i+1}{2}=\binom{i}{2}+i and
(−j2)=(j+12)\binom{-j}{2}=\binom{j+1}{2},
we may compute h0,…,hm−1h_{0},\ldots,h_{m-1}, f0′,…,fn′f^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{n} and
g−n,…,gm−1g_{-n},\ldots,g_{m-1} from α\alpha, α−1\alpha^{-1}
and the coefficients fjf_{j} using O⁡(n+m)O(n+m) multiplications in ℤN\mathbb{Z}_{N}.
For each i∈{0,…,m−1}i\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\},
the sum ∑j=0nfj′​gi−j\sum_{j=0}^{n}f^{\prime}_{j}g_{i-j} in (2.2)
is equal to the coefficient of xix^{i} in
the product of the Laurent polynomials






f′≔∑j=0nfj′​xjandg≔∑k=−nm−1gk​xk.f^{\prime}\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{n}f^{\prime}_{j}x^{j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad g\coloneqq\sum_{k=-n}^{m-1}g_{k}x^{k}.




The problem thus reduces to multiplying a polynomial of degree nn
by a polynomial of degree n+m−1n+m-1,
plus O⁡(n+m)O(n+m) auxiliary multiplications in ℤN\mathbb{Z}_{N}.
The claimed complexity bound then follows from
Lemma 2.1.
∎







2.3. Strassen’s method





The following result,
which goes back to Strassen [Str77],
may be used to quickly rule out “small” factors of a given integer NN.




Proposition 2.5.



Given as input a positive integer M=O⁡(N)M=O(N),
we may test if NN has a prime divisor p⩽Mp\leqslant M,
and if so find the smallest such divisor,
in time






O⁡(M1/2​lg3​N).O(M^{1/2}\lg^{3}N).







Proof.



We give only a sketch; for details (and improvements) see
[Str77], [BGS07] or [CH14].
Let d≔⌈M1/2⌉d\coloneqq\lceil M^{1/2}\rceil
and compute f(x)=(x+1)⋯(x+d)∈ℤN[x]f(x)=(x+1)\cdots(x+d)\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}[x]
using Lemma 2.3.
Then evaluate f⁡(x)f(x) simultaneously at x=0,d,…,(d−1)​dx=0,d,\ldots,(d-1)d,
using a fast multipoint evaluation algorithm.
If any f(jd)=(jd+1)⋯(jd+d)f(jd)=(jd+1)\cdots(jd+d) has non-trivial GCD with NN,
then one of its constituents j​d+ijd+i must have non-trivial GCD with NN,
and this observation may be used to locate any prime divisors of NN
such that p⩽d2p\leqslant d^{2}
(using a similar procedure to the closely related
Algorithm 4.1 of the present paper).
∎





Remark 2.6.



Taking M≔⌊N1/2⌋M\coloneqq\lfloor N^{1/2}\rfloor in the above result leads
directly to Strassen’s N1/4+o⁡(1)N^{1/4+o(1)} integer factorisation algorithm.







2.4. Finding elements of large order





The following result,
which allows us to find elements of large order in ℤN∗\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{*},
is due to Hittmeir.
Of course in practice this is easy, because almost every element of ℤN∗\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{*}
has large order.
The point of Hittmeir’s result is that the algorithm is deterministic
and gives provably correct output.




Proposition 2.7 ([Hit18, Theorem 6.3]).



There is an algorithm with the following properties.
It takes as input integers N⩾2N\geqslant 2 and DD such that
N2/5⩽D⩽NN^{2/5}\leqslant D\leqslant N.
It returns either some α∈ℤN∗\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{*}
such that ordN⁡(α)>D\ord_{N}(\alpha)>D,
or a nontrivial factor of NN, or “NN is prime”.
Its running time is






O⁡(D1/2​lg2​N).O(D^{1/2}\lg^{2}N).








The algorithm is somewhat involved, and we give only a brief summary here.
The idea is to try to compute the orders in ℤN\mathbb{Z}_{N} of candidates
α=2,3\alpha=2,3, and so on, using a BSGS search up to some bound.
If for some candidate α\alpha we fail to compute the order,
then the order must be large and we are done.
On the other hand, if we succeed in computing the order of a candidate,
then this reveals valuable information about the possible prime divisors of NN.
Continuing in this way, Hittmeir shows that eventually we recover enough
information about these divisors that it becomes feasible
to search for them directly using a variant of the algorithm of
Proposition 2.5.
For details, see [Hit18].




Remark 2.8.



According to [Hit18, Remark 6.4],
the assumption D⩾N2/5{D\geqslant N^{2/5}} is not the sharpest possible;
the same result may be proved under the weaker hypothesis
D=Ω⁡(N1/3+o⁡(1))D=\Omega(N^{1/3+o(1)}) for suitable o⁡(1)o(1).
Luckily for us,
the N2/5N^{2/5} bound as stated is good enough for our application
(but only just).








3. Lehman’s strategy





Given an integer NN that is either prime or a semiprime p​qpq,
the main algorithm in Section 4 will attempt to factor NN
by searching for integers of the form a​q+b​paq+bp for various small values
of aa and bb.
Before considering Lehman’s theorem in detail,
we show (following [Hit20, Lem. 4.1]) that it is easy
to recover pp and qq once we have guessed the correct value of a​q+b​paq+bp.




Lemma 3.1.



Given as input an integer N⩾2N\geqslant 2 such that
NN is either a prime or a semiprime p​qpq,
and positive integers a,b,u=O⁡(N)a,b,u=O(N),
we may test if uu is of the form a​q+b​paq+bp,
and if so recover pp and qq,
in time O⁡(𝖬⁡(lg⁡N))O(\MM(\lg N)).





Proof.



If N=p​qN=pq and u=a​q+b​pu=aq+bp, then a​qaq and b​pbp are roots of the
polynomial Q⁡(y)≔y2−u​y+a​b​NQ(y)\coloneqq y^{2}-uy+abN.
We first check if Q⁡(y)Q(y) has rational roots,
by testing whether u2−4​a​b​Nu^{2}-4abN is a square,
and if so, compute the roots via the quadratic formula.

Remark 3.4.



Lemma 3.3 shows that the total number of
candidates for a​q+b​paq+bp,
i.e., the number of integers xx such that

for some kk in the range 1⩽k⩽r1\leqslant k\leqslant r, is at most






∑k=1r⌈N1/24​r​k1/2⌉⩽∑k=1r(N1/24​r​k1/2+1)=O⁡(N1/2r1/2+r).\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left\lceil\frac{N^{1/2}}{4rk^{1/2}}\right\rceil\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left(\frac{N^{1/2}}{4rk^{1/2}}+1\right)=O\left(\frac{N^{1/2}}{r^{1/2}}+r\right).




Lehman optimises this last expression by taking
r≍N1/3=N0.333​…r\asymp N^{1/3}=N^{0.333\ldots},
so that the number of candidates is O⁡(N1/3)O(N^{1/3}).
He checks each one individually,
using the procedure mentioned in Remark 3.2,
leading to his overall N1/3+o⁡(1)N^{1/3+o(1)} bound.





Hittmeir [Hit20] takes a smaller value of rr,
namely r=N2/9+o⁡(1)=N0.222​…+o⁡(1)r=N^{2/9+o(1)}=N^{0.222\ldots+o(1)},
so that the number of candidates increases to
N7/18+o⁡(1)=N0.388​…+o⁡(1)N^{7/18+o(1)}=N^{0.388\ldots+o(1)}.
(Note that in [Hit20], the parameter rr is called η\eta.)
Taking a smaller value of rr introduces more structure
into the space of candidates;
Hittmeir takes advantage of this by using BSGS techniques
to speed up the search instead of examining each candidate individually.
First, for each kk up to N1/9+o⁡(1)=N0.111​…+o⁡(1)N^{1/9+o(1)}=N^{0.111\ldots+o(1)}
(Hittmeir calls this threshold ξ\xi),
he runs a BSGS search to examine all candidates corresponding
to that one value of kk.
Then he performs one more BSGS search to examine all
remaining values of kk up to
r=N2/9+o⁡(1)=N0.222​…+o⁡(1)r=N^{2/9+o(1)}=N^{0.222\ldots+o(1)}.
Altogether, this leads to an overall complexity of N2/9+o⁡(1)N^{2/9+o(1)}.





Our strategy (see Section 4)
is to take rr smaller again,
namely r=N1/5+o⁡(1)=N0.2+o⁡(1)r=N^{1/5+o(1)}=N^{0.2+o(1)}.
The number of candidates increases even further to
N2/5+o⁡(1)=N0.4+o⁡(1)N^{2/5+o(1)}=N^{0.4+o(1)}.
However, we perform a single BSGS search
(similar to the method Hittmeir uses for the large values of kk)
that covers all values of kk simultaneously.
In effect, we enjoy a “square-root speedup” over the entire search space.





An interesting question is whether it is possible to obtain a fully
square-root speedup for Lehman’s original choice r≍N1/3r\asymp N^{1/3}.
This would presumably lead to a factoring algorithm with complexity
N1/6+o⁡(1)N^{1/6+o(1)}.







4. The main algorithm





Before discussing the main search algorithm,
we first consider Algorithm 4.1,
which finds collisions modulo pp or qq between two subsets of ℤN\mathbb{Z}_{N}.
This subroutine is almost identical to
Algorithm 3.2 of [Hit20].
However, whereas Hittmeir allows both subsets to be essentially arbitrary,
we specialise to the case that one of the subsets consists entirely
of powers of some fixed α∈ℤN∗\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{N}.
This allows us to improve the complexity from O⁡((n+m)​lg3​N)O((n+m)\lg^{3}N)
(see [Hit20, Lem. 3.3]) to O⁡(n​lg3​N+m​lg2​N)O(n\lg^{3}N+m\lg^{2}N).

O⁡(s​lg⁡N​lg⁡s+m​lg​N​lg​m)=O⁡((s+m)​lg2​N).O(s\lg N\lg s+m\lg N\lg m)=O((s+m)\lg^{2}N).




Note that the powers αi\alpha^{i} in the first list are distinct thanks
to the hypothesis ordN⁡(α)⩾m\ord_{N}(\alpha)\geqslant m,
so each va,b,jv_{a,b,j} is equal to at most one αi\alpha^{i}.
Linearly traversing the two lists in parallel,
we may find all matches va,b,j=αiv_{a,b,j}=\alpha^{i} in time






O⁡((s+m)​lg⁡N).O((s+m)\lg N).




There are at most ss such matches,
so the cost of invoking Lemma 3.1 for all of them is at most






O⁡(s​lg⁡N​lg⁡lg​N).O(s\lg N\lg\lg N).




Note that the preconditions a,b,u=O⁡(N)a,b,u=O(N) for Lemma 3.1 hold,
because a,b⩽r=O⁡(N)a,b\leqslant r=O(N) and






u=i+j​m+⌈(4​a​b​N)1/2⌉=O⁡(m+N1/24​r​(a​b)1/2+(r​N)1/2)=O⁡(N).u=i+jm+\lceil(4abN)^{1/2}\rceil=O\left(m+\frac{N^{1/2}}{4r(ab)^{1/2}}+(rN)^{1/2}\right)=O(N).







Finally, in Step (4) we certainly have n⩽sn\leqslant s,
so according to Proposition 4.1 the cost of Step (4) is






O⁡(s​lg3​N+m​lg2​N).O(s\lg^{3}N+m\lg^{2}N).







Combining the contributions from Steps (1)–(4), the overall complexity is






O⁡(s​lg3​N+m​lg2​N+r​lg3​N​lg⁡lg⁡N),O(s\lg^{3}N+m\lg^{2}N+r\lg^{3}N\lg\lg N),




and the claimed bound follows immediately.
∎






Gluing the various pieces together,
we obtain Algorithm 4.3
(analogous to [Hit20, Algorithm 6.1]),
which factors any sufficiently large NN known to be a prime or semiprime.




Algorithm 4.3  – factoring (semi)primes




Input: An integer N⩾109N\geqslant 10^{9},
either prime, or a semiprime p​qpq with p<qp<q.




Output: If NN is a semiprime, returns pp and qq. Otherwise returns “NN is prime”.

 




1) 



Set






r≔⌈N1/5lg4/5⁡N⌉⩾1,m≔⌈N1/5​lg6/5​N⌉⩾1.r\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{N^{1/5}}{\lg^{4/5}N}\right\rceil\geqslant 1,\qquad m\coloneqq\lceil N^{1/5}\lg^{6/5}N\rceil\geqslant 1.






2) 



Apply Proposition 2.5 with
M≔⌈(N/r)1/2⌉⩾1M\coloneqq\lceil(N/r)^{1/2}\rceil\geqslant 1.
If any factors are found, return pp and qq.




3) 



Apply Proposition 2.7 with
D≔⌈N2/5⌉⩾1D\coloneqq\lceil N^{2/5}\rceil\geqslant 1.
If any factors of NN are found, or NN is proved to be prime, return.
Otherwise, we obtain α∈ℤN∗\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{*} such that ordN⁡(α)>D\ord_{N}(\alpha)>D.




4) 



Apply Algorithm 4.2 with the given NN, α\alpha, mm and rr.
Return the factors found, or “NN is prime”.







Proposition 4.3.



Algorithm 4.3 is correct, and its running time is






O⁡(N1/5​lg16/5​N).O(N^{1/5}\lg^{16/5}N).







Proof.



In Step (2) we check for prime divisors p⩽Mp\leqslant M.
Since M=O⁡(N2/5​lg2/5​N)M=O(N^{2/5}\lg^{2/5}N),
the cost of invoking Proposition 2.5 is






O⁡((N2/5​lg2/5​N)1/2​lg3​N)=O⁡(N1/5​lg16/5​N).O((N^{2/5}\lg^{2/5}N)^{1/2}\lg^{3}N)=O(N^{1/5}\lg^{16/5}N).




After this, we may assume that NN is either prime,
or a semiprime satisfying (3.1).





In Step (3), observe that certainly N2/5⩽D⩽NN^{2/5}\leqslant D\leqslant N.
The cost of invoking Proposition 2.7 is
O⁡(N1/5​lg2​N)O(N^{1/5}\lg^{2}N), which is negligble.
If Proposition 2.7 does not succeed in factoring NN
or proving NN prime,
we obtain some α∈ℤN∗\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{*} with ordN⁡(α)>D\ord_{N}(\alpha)>D.
As N⩾109N\geqslant 10^{9} we have N⩾(log2⁡N+1)6⩾lg6⁡NN\geqslant(\log_{2}N+1)^{6}\geqslant\lg^{6}N,
so N2/5⩾N1/5​lg6/5​NN^{2/5}\geqslant N^{1/5}\lg^{6/5}N and therefore
ordN⁡(α)>D⩾m\ord_{N}(\alpha)>D\geqslant m.





We have verified the preconditions of Algorithm 4.2,
so Step (4) correctly returns the factorisation of NN.
According to Proposition 4.2,
the cost of this step is






O⁡((N1/2r1/2​m+r)​lg4​N+m​lg2​N)=O⁡(N1/5​lg16/5​N).∎O\left(\left(\frac{N^{1/2}}{r^{1/2}m}+r\right)\lg^{4}N+m\lg^{2}N\right)=O(N^{1/5}\lg^{16/5}N).\qed








Finally we may prove the main theorem, following [Hit20, §6].




Proof of Theorem 1.1.



We are given as input an integer N⩾2N\geqslant 2.
We may reduce to the semiprime case as follows.
We first apply Proposition 2.5 to NN with
M0≔⌈N1/3⌉M_{0}\coloneqq\lceil N^{1/3}\rceil.
If any prime divisor of NN is found, we remove it and denote the resulting
integer by N1N_{1}.
We then apply Proposition 2.5 to N1N_{1}
with M1≔⌈N11/3⌉M_{1}\coloneqq\lceil N_{1}^{1/3}\rceil.
We repeat this process until no more divisors are found.
The number of prime divisors of NN is O⁡(lg⁡N)O(\lg N),
and Mi⩽⌈N1/3⌉M_{i}\leqslant\lceil N^{1/3}\rceil for all ii,
so the cost of this procedure is
O⁡((N1/6​lg3​N)​lg⁡N)=N1/6+o⁡(1)O((N^{1/6}\lg^{3}N)\lg N)=N^{1/6+o(1)},
which is negligible compared to N1/5​lg16/5​NN^{1/5}\lg^{16/5}N.
Let N′N^{\prime} be the integer remaining at the end of the process.
Then 1⩽N′⩽N1\leqslant N^{\prime}\leqslant N and N′N^{\prime} has no more than two prime divisors
(including multiplicities).
We may easily check if N′N^{\prime} is a square in time O⁡(lg⁡N​lg⁡lg​N)O(\lg N\lg\lg N).
If not, then N′N^{\prime} is either a prime or a semiprime.
If N′<109N^{\prime}<10^{9}, we may factor N′N^{\prime} by any desired algorithm
(for example trial division).
Otherwise, we use Algorithm 4.3.
The desired complexity bound follows from Proposition 4.3.
∎
